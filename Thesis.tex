%\section*{\textbf{Summary}}
\begin{abstract}

\textit{The objective of this paper is to analyze  the impact of macroeconomic and bank-specific factors on Non-Performing Loans (NPL) in the EU-banking sector using an extensive, granular dataset on major European banks gathered by the European Banking Authority (EBA) in the context of the EU-wide Transparency Exercise for the period 2014-2019. The analysis takes into consideration disaggregated bank's portfolio characteristics in terms of different exposure types (households, non-financial corporations, financial corporations other than credit institutions), geographic distribution of exposures (worldwide-country of destiny), intensity of cross-border diversification and initial levels of NPL. Moreover, the characteristics of the legal system and the regulatory status under the Single Supervisory Mechanism (SSM) are  also analyzed as potential determinants of NPL. Traditional panel data methodologies (Random Effects/Fixed Effects/GMM) and quantile regression methods for panel data (Pooled/Penalized Fixed Effects) have been applied for the empirical approach. \textbf{The empirical results show the importance of bank-specific and macroeconomic factors as well as the characteristics of the legal system for explaining the development of NPL in the European banking system}. In this context, Common Equity Tier1 Ratio (CET1) and Returns on Risk adjusted Capital (RORAC) are identified as the main bank-specific determinants of NPL, whereas inflation rates, government gross debt as percent of GDP and unemployment rates are found to be the main macroeconomic determinants of NPL. With regard to the characteristics of the legal system, recovery rates  after NPL resolution proceedings are  also found to be an important determinant of NPL. \textbf{The "new" findings also show that the relative importance of the determinants of NPL depends greatly on the exposure type under consideration, the original amount of NPL and the intensity of cross-border diversification in a bank's portfolio}. In this sense, this thesis provides some new empirical evidence that further recognizes the importance of understanding the heterogeneity in the European banking sector for explaining the development of NPL in Europe. \textbf{Furthermore, the "new" findings also suggest that regulation and supervision under the Single Supervisory Mechanism (SSM)  contributes significantly to a reduction of NPL in the European banking system}. In light of these results, the supervisory authorities should further  concentrate efforts on procedures and systems for improving risk management tools at a disaggregated level, thus accounting for the large heterogeneity in the European banking system, while promoting at the same time a further integration of the European financial sector within the established European institutions. Moreover, the presented results are also relevant for the design of recommendations to banks on NPL as published by the ECB and other national supervisory authorities regarding the development and implementation of adequate NPL strategies and Early Warning Indicators (EWI).  \textbf{In this context, the present analysis provides a new example of the potential use of the EBA's data and a new application of the quantile regression methodology for panel data}.}


\end{abstract}

\section{ \textbf{Introduction}}
%\subsection{Motivation}


Since the aftermath of the financial crisis that started in 2008, many European banks were left  facing  large accumulations of Non-Performing Loans (NPL) in their balance sheets. During the last years,  this development have been a very important issue for banks, supervisors and market participants in the European Union.

In 2014, a harmonized definition of NPL across EU Member States was introduced  by the European Banking Authority (EBA), representing the basis for an adequate monitoring and comparability of NPL across European banks. By 2015, around 1 trillion Euros  were reported by European banks  to the European Banking Authority (EBA). In 2017, the European Council for Economic and Financial Affairs (ECOFIN) recognized the importance of providing a bold response to these accumulations of Non-perfoming Loans (NPL) in the European banking sector, thereby pointing out the need of a coordinate action plan both to reduce current levels and to avoid their re-emergence in the future. In this sense, the Council highlighted the need for solutions at European level for a problem that  has until recently  been addressed at national level (\cite{EBA}). Moreover, the expected dramatic economic consequences of the Covid-19 pandemic that started in 2020  have added new concerns on the development of NPL in the European Union, reawakening the interest in this topic.

In this context, a proper understanding of the determinants and evolution of NPL is critical to perform an adequate microprudential monitoring of the banking system. It becomes even more  relevant for macroprudential purposes as the size and evolution of NPL may increase concerns  in terms of systemic risks, such as a threat to the overall financial stability and to the basic role of the banking sector as  financial intermediary and  main provider of credit into the real economy (\cite{Suarez.2018}; \cite{Fell}; \cite{BENDING}). The accumulation of NPL may thus involve severe social costs, specially during and in the aftermath of financial crises. For instance, increasing levels of NPL for longer periods in the banking system are generally followed by a reduction of the credit supply. In this situation, banks are characterized by  having higher funding  and recovery costs as well as limited capability to raise new capital (\cite{Suarez.2018}). Moreover, the presence of large stocks of NPL in a bank's portfolio can also generate negative externalities in relation to borrowers' payment culture. For instance, an increase in the number of borrowers defaulting  their loans  may  create individual incentives for other healthy borrowers not to repay their debts if they perceive that the bank's capabilities to pursue defaulted borrowers  is limited  due to the lack of resources for loan monitoring (\cite{Suarez.2018}).

Since 2016 the EBA  has also been disclosing  for the public detailed bank-by- bank supervisory data  gathered in the context of the EU-wide Transparency Exercise on an annual basis. This data enhances the comparability of problem loans across  European banks by using an harmonized definition of NPL and  providing an extensive granularity (exposures are broken down by banks, asset classes, the largest countries of counterparty, default status and  calculation methods, etc...). Additionally, the quality controls undertaken by the EBA  in the context of  its supervisory competencies ensure high reliability of the provided data (\cite{Kerbl}; \cite{Turk}). 



In the academic research, scholars and international organizations have also pointed out  the presence of some challenges in the NPL literature when using individual bank data despite the need of more disaggregated analyses of NPL drivers (\cite{FSI}; \cite{Cerutti}). These challenges can be summarized in the following points:

\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt, partopsep=0pt,itemsep=0.5 pt,parsep= 0.5 pt]

\item Data is usually provided on a consolidated basis and its availability is limited due to data protection regulation and banking law.
\item Private platforms like Dealscan, Datastream, Bankscope or Debtwire suffers from a representativeness bias and data quality issues
 \item The use of macroeconomic information from the bank's country of origin does not totally account  for cross-border portfolio diversification in the banking sector when analyzing its impact on non-performing loans by using consolidated data at bank-level.
         
\item The lack of comparability of NPL across banks due to divergences concerning the definition of non-performing loans and the method of compilation of the data across countries.

\end {itemize}

Therefore, the main purpose of this thesis is to contribute to a further understanding  of the determinants of  NPL by  analyzing the impact of macroeconomic and bank-specific factors on non-performing loans  in the EU-banking sector using a  granular dataset on major European banks gathered by the European Banking Authority (EBA) in the context of the EU-wide Transparency Exercise for the period 2014-2019. Motivated by the granularity of this dataset,  important aspects of a bank's portfolio, such as different exposure types (households, non-financial corporations, financial corporations other than credit institutions), geographic distribution   of  exposures (worldwide-country of destiny), intensity of  cross-border diversification and initial levels of NPL will be taken into consideration. Moreover, the characteristics of the legal system and the regulatory status under the Single Supervisory Mechanism (SSM) are  also analyzed as potential determinants of NPL.

The use of the EBA data in this thesis is even more  relevant as there are rarely any academic paper  in the Non-Performing Loans (NPL) literature that made any use or reference to the EBA transparency exercises Database despite its high quality standards, the novelty of its compilation and its usefulness to handle certain difficulties concerning the analysis of NPL. More details about its characteristics, advantages and the institutions behind its compilation  will  also be provided in the following sections. For the empirical analysis quantile regression models for panel data  will also be  estimated. These methods provide new insights into  the determinants of  NPL by  allowing a disaggregated estimation of their  impact at different NPL levels along the conditional distribution of NPL in the EU banking sector. 



In summary, the motivation for this present topic stems from  the following reasons:

\begin{itemize}

\item   EU authorities and  the academic literature  claimed that any policy measure and regulatory approach in the European Union with respect to problem loans required a deep understanding of  their underlying determinants

\item  To explore the factors  that contribute to  this understanding, whether those factors have a macroeconomic or bank-specific nature and their impact on NPL

\item  The  availability of  disaggregated supervisory data on major European banks disclosed by the EBA  allows new contributions in the NPL literature, for instance, a detailed analysis of disaggregated NPL-portfolios at bank-level split by exposure type, geographic allocation of exposures and a bank's supervisory status

\item To provide an empirical example of the potential benefits from using the data of the EBA's EU-wide Transparency Exercise for supervisory and academic purposes with respect to certain challenges presented in the NPL literature
        
\item  New developments in the  quantile regression methodology allow a deeper exploration of  the impact of these factors at different quantiles along the conditional distribution of NPL in the EU banking sector

\end{itemize}

\section{\textbf{NPL Literature and Institutional Background }}

\subsection{NPL Literature Review}

 Many studies have shown  particular interest  in the identification of the relationships between bank-specific and macroeconomic determinants and the bank's asset quality, using   non-performing loans data as main indicator. In particular, the literature is divided in two lines of research: one line of research exclusively focusing on the macroeconomic determinants of non-performing loans and other line of research  that also analyze the impact of bank-specific indicators. In this context, macroeconomic conditions are considered as external events that are likely to directly affect the debtor capacity to service loan obligations, while  bank-specific determinants  allows a focus on the factors explaining the variability of non-performing loans across banks.
 
\subsubsection{Linkages between Macroeconomic Indicators and NPL}
 
Regarding the macroeconomic linkages  to bank's loan quality, there is a significant amount of studies. In general, these studies could be subdivided in studies including  general macroeconomic indicators related to the Business cycle (GDP growth, unemployment rates), Terms of trade(current account deficits/surplus, balance of payments) and Price levels (inflation), while other studies also include   government related  indicators such  as sovereign debt and fiscal deficit levels into their models. Finally, a reduced number of papers also analyzes the impact of  indicators related to the overall strength of the legal system, legal protection  of creditors/debtors and supervisory regulation.

In this context, most researchers  identified the importance of the macroeconomic environment in explaining the performance of loan portfolios in the banking sector (\cite{Makri}; \cite{Dimitrios.2016}; \cite{Louzis.2012}). For instance, \citet{Makri} and \citet{Dimitrios.2016} based their hypothesis in the idea that an expansionary phase in the economy  in terms of GDP growth and  low unemployment levels is assumed to improve the ability of economic agents to service
their loans as a sufficient stream of income is provided for the household sector. This situation improves the ability to repay bank loans as well as  boosting private demand, with also positive consequences for the corporate sector. Once the expansionary phase comes to an end, lines of credit are also extended to lower-quality debtors, leading to a progressive increase in defaulted loans. Moreover, if this situation is followed by higher unemployment rates, more borrowers  will be unable to serve current loan obligations, further increasing the amount of defaulted loans in  the bank's portfolio. 

The relation between price levels and non-performing loans have also been researched in several papers (\cite{Salas.2002}; \cite{KleinN.2013}; \cite{NkusuM.2011}). Inflation rates may affect the capacity of borrowers to service their loan obligations in a positive or a negative way, depending on the transmission channel under consideration. On the one hand,  higher inflation rates tend to cut down  the real value of outstanding loans, improving the borrower's capacity to repay loans. On the other hand, these higher rates may also have negative effects on real income  under the assumption that nominal wages are sticky. This last situation may make debt-repayment for borrowers more difficult as real wages and real income are cut down. In this context, the causal relation between inflation rates and non-performing loans can be negative or positive.

%A small numer of papers

A small number of authors, such as \citet{BockR.2012} and \citet{KaminskyGL.1999}, also discuss  the impact of trade related indicators (current account deficits, balance of payments, exchange rates, etc...) on the level of non-perfoming loans as potential drivers of bank asset quality.  For instance, persistent current account surpluses or deficits could be indications of unbalanced economies, relying too much on savings or consumer spending. These indicators can also signal  the lack of competitiveness in the economy of a country in relation to their neighbors.  These issues regarding  asymmetries and macroeconomic imbalances among European countries  in terms of trade and  their effects on the economic and financial instability in the European Union have gained importance in the last years. 


The relation between sovereign debt and financial crises in the banking sector have also been identified in many studies after the  severe financial crisis in 2008  and further sovereign debt developments, in which two  main channels of transmission are considered. First, a worsening of  the finances in the public sector sets limits on the credibility of central banks in the market. This increases the pressure on banks for additional liquidity, leading to a reduction in lending and loan refinancing. Moreover, an excessive increase in sovereign debt may lead to  the need of  severe fiscal measures, potentially affecting social expenditure and public wages. These negative shocks in income have  impacts on the household  and corporate sector by causing a general decrease in demand. These circumstances  may reduce the ability of the economic agents to meet loans obligations (\cite{ReinhartCM.2011}; \cite{BockR.2012})
 

\subsubsection{Rule of Law, Regulation and Supervisory practices}

In recent years, some important studies focused on the question of how regulation and supervisory practices promote banking development, banking performance and  banking stability. For instance, supervisory organizations pointed out the important role of the regulation and monitoring of bank activities as well as the definition of rules regarding  capital ratios as important buffers against financial instability and banking crises. This line of research also estimate the effects of membership to an overall official supervisory power on non-performing loans as the characteristics of the supervisory body have important consequences in bank's risk-taking behaviour and practices. For instance, the independence of the supervisor when defining rules for loan classification, liquidity requirements, definition of guidelines and restrictions on loan practices is an important characteristic to consider as a potential determinant of non-performing loans (\cite{Barth.2004}; \cite{Boudriga}).

In addition,  the legal aspects  concerning lending activities and the overall quality of law enforcement as well as the relative protection of creditors and debtors in loan contract obligations within the legal system   may also have important consequences for debt performance and NPL resolution. In  this regard,   \citet{MoraSanguinetti.2017} and \citet{Rodano.2021} argued that differences in the quality of the enforcement institutions may  impact the levels of non-performing loans in a banks' portfolio due to the fact that fewer loans are  defaulted in countries where efficient declaratory procedures are in place. For instance, variations in the length of time required to recover debt or in the final recovered loan amount, allowing collateral or other assets of the borrower to be repossessed once the procedure for the resolution of non-performing loans concluded, may have important effects on the creditor behaviour for the  loan decision process as well as on the willingness of debtors to meet their contractual obligations. However, these factors may also affect debtor behaviour when deciding to serve its loan contract obligations. For instance, differences in the debt restructuring frameworks and in the laws regulating private sector insolvency in regard to the intensity of the creditor/debtor legal protection may have important consequences for determining the levels of non-performing loans. Furthermore, complex legal frameworks or congested judicial procedures may also discourage the undertaking of legal actions at resolving NPL by foreclosing on available collaterals despite potential losses as mentioned by \citet{Suarez.2018}. Additionally, the existence of an integrated or unified legal framework regulating the accessibility and disposal of credit information and the availability of  data on borrowers' loan repayment behaviour, which is provided by credit bureaus or  public credit registries and used by banks for loan  underwriting purposes, are also relevant factors that may affect the amount of risk in a bank's portfolio and  the levels of non-performing loans.

%\cite{Rodano.2021}
%\citet{Rodano.2021}
% (\cite{Suarez.2018})
In regard to the resolution of NPL, the existence and conditions prevailing in  secondary markets for NPL have also been given special attention in recent times (\cite{Avgouleas.2021}). A secondary market for NPL, where banks can easily sell their NPL to investors, may contribute to accelerate the NPL adjustment process as banks try to restructure portfolios with problem loans. However, some authors argue that one of the main challenges facing this kind of markets is the presence of  asymmetric information. In the case of a secondary market for NPL, these asymmetries mean that potential buyers tend to have less information on the quality of NPL than  the banks selling their NPL stocks and therefore, the buyers will  try to underbid the fair value of the portfolio, with the result that the bank  will either continue the selling operation at depressed prices,  which lead to potential losses, or withdraw the selling offer (\cite{Suarez.2018}). In this context, the need of an  adequate  institutional and legal framework is also essential to reduce some of the negative consequences that may lead to forced sales of NPL portfolios at loss or prevent  that buyers and sellers reach an agreement to sell these at fair value. In other words, the reduction of information asymmetries by establishing a proper regulatory framework for secondary markets of NPL is an important instrument for the reduction of NPL in the  banking sector. In this regard, \citet{Suarez.2018} and \citet{Avgouleas.2021} suggested that the implementation of an  adequate legal framework for the creation and operation  of (public and private) asset management companies (AMCs) specialized in buying and managing NPL in  secondary markets represents a desirable development. In fact, the sales or securitizations in the secondary market of NPL have been shown to be one of the main factors behind the reduction of problem loans, specially for those countries with high levels of NPL (\cite{EBA}).

Finally, structural factors behind the lack of a competitive secondary market in NPL (such as an inadequate legal basis for the establishment and operation of AMCs) may impede or discourage the sale of NPL to third parties.

\subsubsection{Linkages between  Bank-specific Indicators and NPL}

A significant group of papers in the NPL literature  also analyze the impact of bank-specific indicators on the proliferation of non-performing loans. In general, this line of research examined the intersection between a bank's asset quality and other important indicators of bank performance such as cost efficiency, lending policy and bank capitalization. These factors are important elements to consider when evaluating the quality of a bank management and the level of risk  that the organization is prepared to accept for business decisions. In this context, following the initial study of \citet{DeYoung.1997}, several lines of reasoning or hypotheses are presented in the literature  to explain the causal relation between bank-specific factors and the amount of non-performing loans at bank level, namely bad management, skimping, moral hazard, diversification and too big to fail.

Under the "bad management" hypothesis, an increase in non-perfoming loans is found to be positively related with low cost efficiency as a signal of poor senior management practices. Failures in practicing an adequate loan underwriting, poor performance in credit scoring or excessive risk-appetite may lead to grant loans to an elevated proportion of customers with  higher risks of default. Moreover, this hypothesis also covers  the reasoning behind the identification of a correlation between  banks' profitability and NPL as it is assumed that highly profitable banks have fewer incentives to enter into riskier activities, thereby showing  a lesser amount of problem loans. In most papers,  financial soundness indicators such as return on assets (ROA), return on equity (ROE)  or net interest margins (NIM) are used as measures of bank-profitability. Furthermore, the implementation of poor practices in monitoring and controlling borrowers after loans are issued can also be considered as  a bad management practice, for instance, the implementation of poor collection  and/or refinancing measures in order to avoid that loans in arrears surpass the usual threshold of 90 days past-due required for their classification as non-performing loans (\cite{DeYoung.1997}).

Nevertheless, the relationship between cost efficiency and non-performing loans may also be described by a two-way causality mechanism. This idea leads to the "skimping" hypothesis, which  highlights the importance of providing additional  resources  to improve the underwriting, monitoring and control mechanisms in an organization despite the potential increase in measured costs. In this context, high cost efficiency will be positively correlated with the accumulation of non-performing loans. This line of reasoning recognizes the presence of a trade-off between cost efficiency and  the allocation of additional resources for underwriting and monitoring. Banks that put fewer efforts to ensure a better loan quality  may be more cost-efficient in the short-term but show higher levels of non-performing loans in the long-term (\cite{DeYoung.1997}; \cite{Louzis.2012}).


Regarding the "moral hazard" hypothesis, it refers to  the classical "moral hazard" problem when a party takes excessive risks and part of this risk is to  be carried by another party. In the literature, this problem  is usually linked  to concerns regarding bank capitalization. In this line of reasoning, managers in low-capitalized banks have more incentives to undertake riskier lending activities regardless of underwriting and monitoring standards. Furthermore,  better capitalized banks  are considered to operate more efficiently than less capitalized banks because capital adequacy ratios may serve as instruments to control for improper risk-taking behaviour. In resume, a negative causal relation between the degree of bank capitalization and the levels of non-performing loans may be identified in this context (\cite{DeYoung.1997}; \cite{Louzis.2012}; \cite{SinkeyJF.1991}; \cite{GhoshA.2015})

Nevertheless, \citet{KeetonWR.1987} firstly  suggested that this relationship may also show a two-way causality mechanism  by arguing that managers in higher capitalized banks also have the possibility of  implementing more flexible lending policies given a compliance with the respective supervisory regulation. The implementation of more flexible lending policies is also explained by the presence of a  greater buffer  in capital adequacy for undertaking riskier lending activities, which may lead to a major accumulation of non-performing loans in the portfolio, without excessively increasing the risk of insolvency. For the contrary, low-capitalized banks with a capital adequacy ratio under the regulatory minimum may be requested to apply capital adjustments to meet supervisory regulation  that restricts their flexibility in defining lending policies. These adjustments will then take place either by increasing capital, if assuming constant (risk-weighted) assets,  or reducing (risk-weighted) assets, if assuming constant capital. In this situation, banks will likely have to implement more restrictive measures, and therefore, show less willingness to engage in riskier lending activities that may increase the levels of non-performing loans in their portfolio.  Under this last line of reasoning, the causal relation between the degree of bank capitalization and the levels of non-performing loans will be positive as considered in some papers (\cite{Boudriga}; \cite{GhoshA.2017}; \cite{KwanS.1997}; \cite{OseiAssibeyE.2015}).

The strategic aspects of a bank's loan portfolio are also important elements to take into account when identifying  determinants of non-perfoming loans at bank-level as mentioned by \citet{GhoshA.2017} and \citet{Zhang.2016}. For instance, strategic considerations are  in place  when decisions regarding credit-portfolio diversification and specialization are to be made. In general, diversification opportunities in the banking sector are considered to be positively related with loan quality as more diversified portfolios are assumed to carry less credit risks. Some authors also use bank size as a proxy for diversification opportunities, identifying a negative relation between bank size and non-performing loans (\cite{Salas.2002}). 

Regarding bank size, \citet{BergerA.N.&DeYoungR.2001} pointed out the potential gains in terms of bank efficiency  with greater economies of scale as it may be the case for larger banks. In this sense, larger banks are  expected to show lower ratios of non-performing loans in their portfolios.

However, bank size has also been related to concerns  on the presence of moral hazard in the too-big-to-fail problem, which represents another potential channel to explain non-performing loans at bank-level. In this logic, larger banks have more incentives to take excessive risks under the assumption that due to their size, complexity and interconnectedness with other economies, they are more likely to be bailed out by the government in case of bank failure or insolvency.

In these cases, traditional market penalties imposed by creditors and other market participants are not properly taken into consideration when forming business decisions. This situation leads to an excessive willingness to engage in risky lending practices under the belief that a potential failure is to be avoided by government protection due to the threat that it represents for the integrity of the financial system and of the national economy as a whole. In other words, banks, whose failure or insolvency may represent a  systemic risk for the  overall financial stability,  may also show higher proportions of non-perfoming loans in their portfolios (\cite{Louzis.2012}). In this view, bank size is assumed to be positively related to  non-performing loans.


\subsubsection{Geographic Diversification and Disaggregated Loan Aspects }

There are few papers that assess the effects of geographic diversification on bank efficiency. According to traditional portfolio theory, the main advantage of portfolio diversification is the reduction of potential losses due to over concentration. Geographic diversification is a technique that aims at reducing risks under the assumption that resources allocated in different regions will react differently  to the same event (\cite{BergerA.N.&DeYoungR.2001}). For these reasons, an increase in geographic diversification is expected to lead to a reduction of the risk of financial loss and, therefore, to lower levels of non-perfoming loans in the portfolio. Moreover, geographic diversification in banking  may facilitate the flow of internal cash through the organization, thereby reducing the need of external funding costs (\cite{Morgan.2016}). In addition, geographic diversification may also benefit from the presence of economies of scale as explained by the fact that it is easier for larger banks to allocate resources  beyond their own country of origin. In a regulatory context, the intensity of geography diversification is also important  because the existence of  heterogeneities in the bank supervisory and regulatory framework may create incentives to diversify or concentrate loan portfolios in one or several countries/regions. In this situation, the presence of different regulatory and risk-control requirements for banks regarding loan exposures in countries other  than the country of origin may have implications for the overall bank's asset quality in a consolidated basis.

However, geographic diversification may also create additional costs due to  problems related to the learning curve and agency costs that may cause potential inefficiencies in the business decision process as well as in the monitoring and controlling of borrowers in cross-border economies. As banks tend to diversify their portfolio by entering into new markets, the collection of soft information  becomes more difficult because the distance between headquarters and subsidiaries  increases and the transmission of information across management layers becomes more challenging, thereby deteriorating the effectiveness of banks' monitoring activities and increasing  overall agency costs (\cite{Morgan.2016}). Furthermore, \citet{Hayden.2006} suggested that banks' decision to enter into a country with an already well-developed banking sector may cause an  adverse selection problem in the pool of borrowers. For instance, foreign banks in already well established  banking sectors may have more difficulties to differentiate  new borrowers from old borrowers, who have been rejected by their previous bank, than national banks fully operating in their home market. In this sense, banks with greater geographical diversification are more affected by the presence of  asymmetric information and adverse selection problems with respect to borrowers than less geographically diversified banks. In this line of reasoning, an increase in the cross-border diversification of a  bank's portfolio may be positively correlated with a higher amount of non-performing loans (\cite{Hayden.2006}).

Despite the fact that the largest part of the academic literature explored the determinants of NPL from an aggregate view, \citet{NkusuM.2011}, \citet{GhoshA.2017} and \citet{ManzFlorian} also recognized the importance of considering different loan types when analyzing the determinants of non-performing loans. This consideration is justified by the fact that different weights of one or more specific sectors in a bank's portfolio composition may play an important role in explaining the amount of NPL in a consolidated basis  (\cite{KeetonWR.1987}). Different loan types face different credit risks and react differently to specific sector developments, thus also reflecting  differences in the potential amount of non-performing loans due to external and internal events (\cite{Salas.2002}). In this context, an analysis of the impact of NPL determinants in a bank portfolio from a disaggregated perspective in terms of loan type is also favoured  by a part of the NPL literature.
 

\subsubsection{Empirical methods and Regional Scope}

With regard to the empirical methods, there is a vast amount of models and methodologies available in the literature. The most common applied methodologies cover static and dynamic panel models such as Random/Fixed Effects and Generalized Method of Moments (GMM) estimations, Granger-causality techniques and logit regressions. Additionally,  the use of Vector Autoregression Regression methods (VAR)  in this line of research has also increased during the last years given that long panel datasets with many time periods are also getting more easily available as observed by \citet{ManzFlorian}. Most recently, some papers have also been published that  make  some use of quantile regression methods in  related topics, although very few in nature. In this sense, \citet{SanchezSerrano.2021} analyzed  the impact of NPL on bank lending by using pooled quantile regression methodology and data from the EBA Transparency Exercises. Also in recent times, \citet{Karadima.2020} applied a penalized quantile regression model for dynamic panel data  in an attempt to analyze the impact of market power in NPL with data obtained from the Orbis BankFocus (BankScope) Database.
 %\cite{ManzFlorian}
The available literature also  provides many studies covering different  regions  and countries, with some papers focusing on specific countries while others expanding their analyses to a group of countries or regions. There also are differences in the source and level  of aggregation of the data, which vary greatly among papers. Most studies  have tended to use aggregated data, mainly driven by the fact that  aggregated  data at country level is more easily available than data at bank level, whose use for academic research is subject to more restrictions due to data protection regulations and bank law. In this context, studies using data at bank level are either complied to subscribe private database providers subject to the payment of fees such as  the Orbis BankFocus (BankScope), Dealscan, Datastream among others, or to use data gathered by national central banks or institutions, which is usually not public  and only available to specific circle of researchers, central bank employees or subject to authorization procedures.  In this situation, the replicability of these studies  is quite challenging and, therefore, any assertion about their reliability under scientific standards may easily be put in doubt. Furthermore, the NPL literature also varies greatly in terms of the time period under consideration (pre-crisis and post-crisis) as well as the periodicity of the data (yearly, quarterly, semesterly).

\subsubsection{The European Banking Authority (EBA)}
 
The European Banking Authority (EBA) is an independent EU Authority established on 1 January 2011 as part of the European System of Financial Supervision (ESFS) to ensure effective and consistent prudential regulation and supervision across the European banking sector. 
%\cite{NPL2} \cite{SSM.Manual.2018} \cite{EBA}

With the EU-wide Transparency Exercise, the EBA has been disclosing supervisory data on  an annual basis since 2016 that provides the public and market participants with updated (quarterly/semesterly) information on the financial conditions of EU banks at the highest level of consolidation. This data is characterized by its extension and granularity  with portfolio-by-portfolio financial items with respect to major European banks. In particular, the EU-wide Transparency Exercise contains detail information about loan exposures, asset classes, default status and important balance sheet items broken down by bank, and it covers between 80\% and 85\% of banking assets in the European Union (\cite{EBA6}; \cite{guideline}). These exercises mainly aim to foster market discipline in the European single market and enhance transparency in the EU banking sector. 

Furthermore, the  data from the EU-wide Transparency Exercise makes use of a harmonized definition of NPL across European countries implemented by the EBA technical standards and published in 2014 by the European Banking Authority (EBA) for supervisory reporting of non-performing loans and forbearance (\cite{NPL2}). These technical standards  represent a  first time attempt to create a hard-law instrument  that establishes a common definition of NPL by providing  a common framework across the EU to accommodate and to harmonize differing national practices without modifying accounting and regulatory standards. The new common framework significantly improves the comparability of NPL across individual banks operating in the  European banking and financial sector.

According to the EBA's EU-harmonized definition, a non-performing exposure is in general an exposure that is 90 days past-due (material exposure) or unlikely to be repaid in full without collateral realization (irrespective of any past-due amount or of the number of days past-due), or impaired/defaulted according to the applicable accounting or regulatory framework (\cite{NPL2}).

In recent times, the lack of comparability of NPL across countries has been a major concern as expressed by many  international organizations such as the Bank for International Settlements (BIS), the Financial Stability Board (FSB), the Basel Committee on
Banking Supervision (BCBS) and the International Monetary Fund (IMF). These concerns are explained by the presence of significant divergences regarding the classification system, scope, and contents of NPL across countries and by the fact that the criteria  defined in domestic laws and applied by banks for designating a loan as non-performing is  to a large extent discretionary. In general, the main factors explaining divergences in NPL definitions  across domestic laws are related to (1) the required number of past-due dates before a loan is considered a non-performing loan (2) the "extension" of  affected loans, that is whether the categorization  of a loan as non-performing loan is to be extended to all outstanding loans  from the same borrower in the same bank regardless of their performing status (3) the role played by collaterals in the non-performing criteria and valuation (4) the minimum amount of time for a "restructured or cured" non-performing loan before the reclassification as performing loan takes place again (\cite{Platon}).

After the financial and sovereign crises, there were many concerns regarding asset quality and non-performing loans management across the EU-banking sector due to the lack of comparability of NPL figures as reported by the national supervisory authorities. This situation made difficult  the achievement of a common understanding, the implementation of an adequate risk assessment and the design of solving strategies for banks with cross-border exposures in the  European Union (EU). In this context, the implementation of the EBA technical standards in 2014  meant a step forward towards larger levels of  convergence  and comparability within the EU-banking sector.

\subsubsection{The Single Supervisory Mechanism (SSM)}

The Single Supervisory Mechanism (SSM) represents the system of banking supervision for all European Union (EU) member states that are part of the Economic and Monetary Union (EMU), which became fully operational on November 4th 2014, as the first pillar of the European banking union, and it is composed of the European Central Bank (ECB) and the national supervisory authorities of the EU member states.

The SSM seeks to protect financial stability and assist the integration of the European financial sector with the support of the established European institutions and in conjunction with the Single Resolution Mechanism (SRM). The Single Resolution Mechanism (SRM) complements the SSM by centralizing  key competences and resources for managing the failure of banks subject to SSM supervision  in case of facing serious financial difficulties (\cite{SSM.Manual.2018}).

The SSM also provides the legislative and institutional framework that gives the European Central Bank (ECB) sole licensing authority and prudential supervisory competencies over all banks operating in any of the EU member states (except branches of banks from non-European Economic Area (EEA) countries), directly for the most important credit institutions ("significant" banks) and indirectly for the rest of credit institutions ("less significant" banks). The significance of a bank is assessed based on size, significance for the economy of any participating Member State and importance of cross-border activities as established in the SSM Regulation. All countries  in the euro area are required to participate, while participation is voluntary for non-eurozone EU member states (\cite{lex}; \cite{SSM.Manual.2018}).

One of the main priorities for the European Central Bank (ECB) banking supervision is  to  address asset quality issues. The main focus of the ECB on non-performing loans started in 2014 after a comprehensive assessment that  established as main pillars the implementation of asset quality reviews and stress tests. The ECB banking supervision  has since then continued to intensify its supervisory  activities  regarding  NPL monitoring. In this context, the ECB also publishes guidances to banks on NPL that provide recommendations on the development of NPL strategies for all "significant" banks supervised directly under the Single Supervisory Mechanism (SSM) (\cite{guideline}).

In general,  these guidances point out that the development and implementation of an NPL strategy should consider internal factors such as a correct assessment of internal capabilities and its capital implications, external factors such as the operating and macroeconomic environment and  regulatory factors such as the legal and judicial framework (\cite{guideline}).

 \subsection{Cross-Border Exposure-Weighted Macroeconomic Indicators}
 
 To determine the macroeconomic drivers on NPL  for  banks with  a cross-border portfolio using data provided on a domestically controlled consolidation basis, the use of exposure-weighted macroeconomic indicators, which account for the geographic composition of exposures in portfolio according to  their country of destiny, was required  as suggested by \citet{Quagliariello} and \citet{Fuchs}. This is possible because the EBA Transparency Exercise also provided data on original exposures up to the 10 largest countries of counterparty at bank level, covering over 85\% of bank assets.

For the calculation of cross-border exposure-weighted macroeconomic indicators, the calculation approach presented by \citet{Quagliariello} was followed:

\begin{align*}
y^\ast_{it} = \sum_{l=0}^{N_{it}} w^t_{il}Y_{lt} \qquad \text{with}\qquad \sum_{l=0}^{N_{it}} w^t_{il}= 1
\end{align*}
\\
where $w^t_{il}$ is the cross-border exposure-weight of bank $i$ from country $l$ at time  $t$, $Y_{lt}$ is the macroeconomic variable of country $l$ at time $t$ and $y^\ast_{it}$ is the cross-border exposure-weighted macroeconomic indicator for bank $i$ at time $t$. The  value of $N_{it}$  varies depending on the number of countries with cross-border exposures for each bank $i$ at time $t$ with $\max(N)= 10$.

\subsection{Cross-Border Diversification Index $(CBDI)$}

A geographic diversification index of bank exposures is also introduced into the model in order to account for the effects of cross-border diversification  on non-performing loans. The Cross-Border Diversification Index ($CBDI$) has been calculated as the inverse of the Hirschmann-Herfindahl Index (HHI) for each bank $i$ at time $t$ as suggested by \citet{Morgan.2016}. Formally,


\[
 CBDI_{it} = 1 - HHI_{it}
\]
with
\[
  HHI_{it} =\sum_{j=0}^{N_{it}}( \dfrac{X_{ijt}}{X_{it}})
\]

Where $X_{j}$ measures  the original exposure towards country $j$ and $N_{it}$ represents the number of countries with the largest cross-border exposures for each bank $i$ at time $t$ with $\max(N)= 10$. The Hirschmann-Herfindahl Index is bounded by $1/N_{it} \leq HHI_{it} \leq 1$. A Hirschmann-Herfindahl Index ($HHI$) close to one means that the bank's original exposures are very concentrated on certain countries whereas an index close to its minimum means that the banks' original exposures are very diversified across countries (\cite{Schertler.2006}; \cite{Hayden.2006}; \cite{BergerA.N.&DeYoungR.2001}). The Cross-Border Diversification Index ($CBDI$) is bounded by $0 \leq CBDI_{it} \leq (N_{it} -1)/N_{it}$. A Cross-Border Diversification Index ($CBDI$) close to its maximum means that the bank's original exposures are very diversified across countries   whereas an index close to zero means that the bank's original exposures are very concentrated on certain countries (\cite{Morgan.2016}). The calculations are based on data taken from the same item used for determining cross-border exposures as described in the previous point.

The Cross-Border Diversification Index ($CBDI$) has been calculated as  the inverse of the the Hirschmann-Herfindahl Index (HHI) for each bank $i$ at time $t$ as suggested by Morgan and Samolyk (2016) \cite{Morgan.2016}. Formally,
% 
% \[
%  CBDI_{it} = 1 - HHI_{it}
% \]
% with
% \[
%   HHI_{it} =\sum_{j=0}^{N_{it}}( \dfrac{X_{ijt}}{X_{it}})
% \]
% 
% where $X_{j}$ measures  the original exposure towards country $j$  and $N_{it}$ represents the number of countries with the largest cross-border exposures for each bank $i$ at time $t$ with $\max(N)= 10$. The Hirschmann-Herfindahl indexes are bounded by $1/N_{it} \leq HHI_{it} \leq 1$. A Hirschmann-Herfindah Index ($HHI$) close to one means that the bank original exposures are very concentrated on certain countries whereas an index close to its minimum means that the bank original exposures are very diversified across countries \cite{Schertler.2006} \cite{Hayden.2006}. The Cross-Border Diversification Indexes ($CBDI$) are bounded by $0 \leq CBDI_{it} \leq (N_{it} -1)/N_{it}$. A Cross-Border Diversification Index ($CBDI$) close to its maximum means that the bank original exposures are very diversified across countries   whereas an index close to zero means that the bank original exposures are very concentrated on certain countries \cite{Morgan.2016}. The calculations are based on data taken from the same item used for determining cross-border exposures as described in previous point.

\subsection{ "Standard" Panel Data Methods}

A (balanced) panel data set differs from a regular time-series or cross-section regression in that  multiple observations on each individual  over time are provided in the sample (\cite{Baltagi.2005}), that is: 

\[
y_{it} = \alpha  + X'_{it}\beta + u_{it} \quad i=1,..., N;\enspace t=1,..., T
\]

with $i$ denoting  individuals and $t$ denoting time. In our case, $it$ is the observation corresponding to the $i$th bank in year $t$, thus the $i$ subscript represents the cross-section dimension and the $t$ represents the time-series dimension. The dependent variable $y_{it}$ represents the non-performing ratio for bank $i$ in year $t$, \alpha$ is a scalar, $\beta$ is $K \,\times\, 1$ and $X_{it}$ is the \textit{it}th observation on $K$ explanatory variables. The $K$ explanatory variables consist of bank-specific and macroeconomic variables.

The models used in the literature for defining the disturbance term $u_{it}$ are classified as one-way error component model or two-way error component model. In the present case, a one-way error component model for the disturbance term $u_{it}$ will be assumed.

A one-way error component model is defined as:

\[
u_{it} = \mu_{i} + \upsilon_{it}
\]

where $\mu_{i}$ denotes the unobservable  individual bank-specific effects and $\upsilon_{it}$ denotes the remainder disturbance (\cite{Baltagi.2005}). This is the case in most panel data applications.

The  use of specific panel models in this paper presents several benefits over  other standard models such as cross-section or time series (\cite{Hsiao.2010}; \cite{Klevmarken.1989}; \cite{Baltagi.2005}):

\begin{enumerate} [topsep=0pt, partopsep=0pt,itemsep=0.5 pt,parsep= 0.5 pt]

\item \textit{These models explicitly address unobserved individual heterogeneity across banks and periods and thereby allowing for controls over  unobserved state- and time-invariant variables that possibly lead to biased estimators whereas this is not  the case in time-series or cross-sectional models}

\item \textit{Micro panel data at bank level  may provide more precise measurements than similar variables at the macro level. Potential bias due to aggregation over individual data may decrease or  be fully excluded}

\item \textit{Panel data provided  more information, less collinearity among the variables and more variability, which lead to an improvement of efficiency}

\item \textit{Panel data is more adequate to study the dynamics of adjustment over time than cross-sectional  data because cross-sectional distributions  may hide a multitude of interesting aspects}

\item \textit{Panel data is better to identify and measure  certain effects not detectable
in mere cross-sectional or time-series analysis}

\item \textit{Panel data models allow the construction and testing of more sophisticated models using microdata with multiple time periods}

\end{enumerate}

The description of the below models corresponds to the standard case where the panel data is assumed to be complete, that is in the case where all individuals are observed over the entire sample period. Nevertheless, incomplete data is also handled  in the present analysis, in so far as the exact same set of banks are not necessarily covered for each time period because the individual requirements and circumstances that a bank has to meet for its participation in the EBA transparency exercise may change from year to year. This typical scenario leads to  an "unbalanced" or "incomplete" panel (\cite{Baltagi.2005}). In general, an unbalanced panel data set is one in which individuals may be observed different numbers of times. This can be notated as  a block of observations $T_{i}$ where the total number of observations are defined as $\sum_{n=1}^N T_{n}$. Although there are certain technical differences that require more tedious matrix algebra, the models described above can also be implemented for the case of unbalanced panel data (\cite{Green.2012}; \cite{Croissant2}). 


      	\subsubsection{ Fixed Effects (FE) and Random Effects (RE) Models}
      	
      	In this approach the time-invariant individual effects ($\mu_{i}$, $\lambda_{it}$) are explicitly introduced into the econometric specification as fixed parameters. For our analysis it implies that these unobserved effects  corresponding to each individual bank may or may not correlate with the independent variables used for the estimation, leading to  unbiased and inconsistent estimators. The remainder disturbances ($\upsilon_{it}$)  are assumed to be independent and identically distributed IID$(0,\,\sigma_{\upsilon}^2)$. The $X_{it}$ are assumed to be uncorrelated with the stochastic disturbances $\upsilon_{it}$ for all $i$ and $t$ (\cite{Baltagi.2005}). In order to remove these unobserved individual effects across banks, an FE model can be defined  through different techniques (LSDV Methods, First-Differences or FD estimator) (\cite{Croissant2}).
      	
      	In the LSDV estimation, the time-invariant individual components ($\mu_{i}$) are explicitly estimated in the model in the form of individual intercepts given the presence of a second dimension (time). In this case, the degrees of freedom will decrease to $NT-N-K$ due to the extra parameters. This method may suffer a large loss of degrees of freedom for cases where $N$ are very large as it is the case in the present analysis. The estimation of $N-1$ extra parameters and several dummies may worse the problem of multicollinearity among the independent variables (\cite{Baltagi.2005}). Another FE method is the so called First-Differences or FD estimator, in which the state-invariant components can be eliminated by first differencing the data, that is  by lagging  and subtracting the variables the intercept and the  individual error components ($\mu_{i}$) are removed.
      	
As mentioned previously, the LSDV Method suffers from a potentially large number of estimates in the basic regression. A statistically equivalent and more compact method for obtaining the same estimators is achieved by performing a data transformation, in which the average over time per individual is subtracted to each variable. This represents the so called Within regression, and it has become the standard method to perform  estimations under a fixed effects model with individual effects. This method is the one selected for the Fixed Effects estimations with static panel data. It is defined as:
% \begin{align*}
%  &  y_{it}-\bar{y}_{i}= (x_{it}-\bar{x}_{i})\beta + (u_{it}-\bar{u_{i}})  &
% \end{align*}
% where $\bar{y}_{i}$ and $\bar{x}_{i}$ are the individuals means of $y$ and $X$. This is equivalent to the regression model
% \begin{align*}
% & y_{it} = \beta' x_{it} +  u_{it} &
% \end{align*}

\[
 y_{it}-\bar{y}_{i}= (x_{it}-\bar{x}_{i})\beta + (u_{it}-\bar{u_{i}}) 
\]

where $\bar{y}_{i}$ and $\bar{x}_{i}$ are the individuals means of $y$ and $X$. This is equivalent to the regression model:

\[
y_{it} = \beta' x_{it} +  u_{it} 
\]
  
where the individual intercepts are free to vary while still being considered  valid parameters for estimation. These are also easily recovered  from the OLS estimation of the within transformation data.

      	%	\subsubsection{Random Effects Model}
      		
A typical issue in the Fixed Effects models is the danger of a large loss of degrees of freedom due to the incorporation of too many parameters. This issue can be avoided by assuming that the individual effects $\mu_{i}$ are random events. In that case, $\mu_{i}\;\sim$IID$(0,\,\sigma^2_{\mu})$, $\upsilon_{it}\;\sim$IID$(0,\,\sigma^2_{\upsilon})$ and $\mu_{i}$ are independent of $\upsilon_{it}$. Furthermore, $X_{it}$ are independent of  $\mu_{i}$ and $\upsilon_{it}$ for all $i$ and $t$, whereas in the Fixed Effects model the unobserved individual effects ($\mu_{i}$) are allowed to correlate with the included variables ($X_{it}$). The Random Effects model is appropriate for cases where $N$ is large, as in the present analysis, because it greatly reduces the number of parameters to be estimated compared to the other model, which may lead to a large loss of degrees of freedom. In this case, the inference of the individual effects is assumed to be random realizations of a sample taken from a larger population (\cite{Baltagi.2005}). The potential cost for this model would be the possibility of inconsistent estimators in case the assumption of randomness turns out to be inappropriate (\cite{Green.2012}). 
 
Additionally, several tests for individual effects, serial correlation, correlated effects and multicollinearity will also be applied in the empirical analysis.

      %\subsubsection{Tests for Individual Effects, Serial Correlation, Correlated Effects and Multicollinearity}
      	
      
      	In order to test whether individual effects are present, the hypothesis test should be constructed in such a way that the null hypothesis represents the assumption that there are not significant individual effects across observations and, therefore, there is no ground  to a preference of panel data techniques over simple Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) estimation.  In this paper, the Breusch-Pagan Lagrange Multiplier Test will be used to test for individual effects.  The  main idea is to start with the OLS regression model and infer whether unobserved time-invariant effects can be detected from the OLS residuals (\cite{Croissant2}). More specifically, the Breusch-Pagan Lagrange Multiplier corresponds to test:
 \medskip
          \begin{itemize}[label=$\bullet$,topsep=0pt, partopsep=0pt,itemsep=0.5 pt,parsep= 0.5 pt]
              \item $H_{0}$:$\sigma^2_{\mu} = 0$
            
              \item $H_{1}$: $\sigma^2_{\mu} \neq 0$
              
           \end{itemize}
\medskip  
In this case, the null hypothesis $H_{0}$ means that there is not significant variances across individuals (absence of individual
effects). Moreover, as the presence of panel effects can not be identified, there are not  justification for the use of panel techniques over OLS in order to control for individual heterogeneity.


Additionally, an F test for the presence of individual effects that compares the Ordinary Least-Squares and the Fixed Effects model  is also useful.  Under the null of no individual effects, the statistic is distributed as F with degrees of freedom equal to the number of individual banks minus 1 on the numerator and to the degrees of freedom of the Fixed Effects Model on the denominator.

       Furthermore, the errors in models  with individual effects are always serially correlated due to the presence of time-invariant unobserved effects in the error components that do not decline over time. Additionally, serial correlation that die out over time may also be present in the errors in the sense of an \textbf{AR(1)} process. In order to test for this aspect, Unobserved Effects and Fixed Effects (FE) test will be applied.

      		In fact, the main argument that determines whether a random or fixed effects model is more appropriate for a specific econometric analysis is the possibility of correlation between the covariates and the individual effects. Maintaining the assumption that the stochastic disturbances $\upsilon$ are uncorrelated with the covariates ($\mathbb{E}[X'\,\upsilon]= 0$), two situations may take place (\cite{Croissant2}):
\medskip
              \begin{itemize}[label=$\bullet$,topsep=0pt, partopsep=0pt,itemsep=0.5 pt,parsep= 0.5 pt]
              \item $\mathbb{E}[X'\,\mu]= 0$: the individual effects are uncorrelated and both models are consistent but a Random Effects estimator is more efficient than a Fixed Effects estimator.
            
              \item $\mathbb{E}[X'\,\mu]\neq 0$: the individual effects are correlated. This implies that only a Fixed Effects model provides consistent estimates because the within estimation eliminates all unobserved time-invariant effects.
              \end{itemize}
\medskip  
In this paper, the Hausman test approach will be used in order to test for correlated effects. The general principle of the Hausman test in panel models consists in comparing  the  estimates of the Fixed Effects (FE) model and the Random Effects (RE) model by performing the following hypothesis test:
\medskip
          \begin{itemize}[label=$\bullet$,topsep=0pt, partopsep=0pt,itemsep=0.5 pt,parsep= 0.5 pt]
              \item Under $H_{0}$: FE and RE are consistent, but RE is more efficient than FE.
            
              \item Under $H_{1}$: Only FE is consistent
              
           \end{itemize}
\medskip
The idea of the test is that if  the null hypothesis $H_{0}$ is true, then the estimated coefficients from FE and RE should not be significantly different, whereas under the alternative hypothesis the estimated coefficients would diverge. Therefore, the test is based on $\hat{\beta}_{FE} - \hat{\beta}_{RE}$ where under $H_{0}$ the variance is $\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\beta}_{FE} - \hat{\beta}_{RE})= \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\beta}_{FE})-\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\beta}_{RE})$ (\cite{Croissant2}). The common hypothesis test  will be  the version where:
      
\[
   \hat{q}=\hat{\beta}_{FE} - \hat{\beta}_{RE}
\]

with variance:

\[
   \mathrm{Var}(\hat{q})= \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\beta}_{FE})+ \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\beta}_{RE})-2\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\beta}_{FE},\hat{\beta}_{RE}) 
\]

%Under the hypotesis of no correlation between independent variables and the error disturbances.
The hypothesis test is then formalized as: 
 \medskip
             \begin{itemize}[label=$\bullet$,topsep=0pt, partopsep=0pt,itemsep=0.5 pt,parsep= 0.5 pt]
       
              \item  $H_{0}$: $\hat{q} = 0$
            
              \item $H_{1}$: $\hat{q} \neq 0$
              
              \end{itemize}
 \medskip
 


Moreover, a standard assumption in the estimation is that the explanatory variables are not perfectly correlated with each other. If two independent variables are very highly correlated, then the  variance  of the corresponding coefficient is very high, tending to infinite if the correlation is perfect. This is known as a problem of multicollinearity, in which coefficients may have very high standard errors and low significance even if these are  jointly significant and the $R^2$ of the estimation is very high or some of the coefficients may show implausible or negative values (\cite{Green.2012}).

% \begin{itemize}[label=$-$,topsep=0pt, partopsep=0pt,itemsep=0.5 pt,parsep= 0.5 pt]
% \item Small  variations in the data  produce wide swings in the coefficient estimates.
% \item Coefficients may have very high standard errors and low significant even if these are  jointly significant and the $R^2$ fo the estimation is very high.
% \item Some of the coefficient may show implausible or negative values.
% 
% \end{itemize}

In this paper, the  Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) will be used  to test for multicollinearity. The Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) is determined by $1/(1-R^2_{k})$ where $R^2_{k}$ is the $R^2$ in the regression of the independent variable $x_{k}$ on all other independent variables included in the model. Thus, the VIF will be determined for each independent variable $x_{k}$ in the main model. The standard benchmark  is  that  VIF values higher than 10 are a signal of  the presence of multicollinearity (\cite{Green.2012}).
	

 \subsubsection{ Generalized Method of Moments (GMM) Models}

	To understand better the dynamics of non-performing loan ratios, certain dynamic panel models developed in the framework of the Generalized Method of Moments (GMM)  approach will also be used in the econometric analysis (\cite{Baltagi.2005}). These models are dynamic because one of the covariates is the lagged dependent variable, in our case the  lagged values of NPL. The dynamic relationship of NPL ratios over time are estimated for  a large sample of individual banks observed over a small period of years (2014-2019). The dynamic panel models  are very well suited for this kind of microdata where $N >> T$ (\cite{Croissant2}). This model is usually formalized as:
	
\[
y_{it} = \delta y_{i,t-1}  + x'_{it}\beta + u_{it} \quad i=1,..., N;\enspace t=1,..., T
\]

where $\delta$ is a scalar, $x'_{it}$ is $1 \,\times\, K$ and $\beta$ is $K \,\times\, 1$, and $ u_{it}$ follows a one-way component model:

\[
u_{it} = \mu_{i} + \upsilon_{it}
\]

where $\mu_{i}\;\sim$IID$(0,\,\sigma^2_{\mu})$ and $\upsilon_{it}\;\sim$IID$(0,\,\sigma^2_{\upsilon})$, and the error disturbances $\upsilon_{it}$ are assumed to be mutually uncorrelated $\mathbb{E}(\upsilon_{it}\, \upsilon_{is})= 0\,\, \forall s \neq t$ and not correlated with the individual effects $\mathbb{E}(\mu_{i}\, \upsilon_{it})= 0$. In our analysis, these models include time-invariant individual effects represented derived from the heterogeneity among individual banks as well as autocorrelation related to the use of a lagged endogenous variable (NPL ratio) in the regressors (\cite{Baltagi.2005}; \cite{Croissant2}).\\

Given that $y_{it}$ is a function of $\mu_{i}$ it can be derived that $y_{i,t-1}$ is also a function of $\mu_{i}$. The result is a biased and inconsistent OLS estimator. The bias of the OLS is caused by the correlation between the lagged endogenous variables and the disturbances due to the existence of individual effects. In the OLS estimation, the estimator is proved to be positively biased while in the Fixed Effects estimation, the estimator is negatively biased (\cite{Croissant2}). In this situation, a consistent coefficient is expected to be comprised between that of a OLS and a Fixed Effects estimation (\cite{Croissant2}; \cite{Baltagi.2005}). Several consistent estimation methods for dynamic panel models are presented in the literature (\cite{Anderson.Hsiao.1982}; \cite{Ahn.Schmidt.1995}). In this paper, the method proposed by \citet{Arellano.Bond.1991} and eventually its extended versions (\cite{Arellano.Bover.1995}; \cite{Blundell.Bond.1998}) will be considered  due to their larger efficiency in this analysis.

The main method is the so-called Difference GMM Estimator, which was firstly developed by \citet{Arellano.Bond.1991} as the further extension of the instrumental variable (IV) approach developed by Anderson and Hsiao in 1982. \citet{Anderson.Hsiao.1982}  suggested differencing  of both the dependent and independent variables to get rid of $\mu_{i}$ and then instrument the lagged endogenous variables in differences, $\Delta y_{i,t-1} = (y_{i,t-1} - y_{i,t-1})$ with the endogenous variables in levels lagged by two periods $y_{i,t-2}$. These instruments will not be correlated with $\Delta \upsylon_{i,t-1} = (\upsilon_{i,t-1} - \upsilon_{i,t-1})$  as long as the $\upsilon_{it}$  are not serially correlated. Although this method generates consistent estimates, these are not necessarily efficient as observed by \citet{Baltagi.2005} because:

\begin{itemize} [topsep=0pt, partopsep=0pt,itemsep=0.5 pt,parsep= 0.5 pt]
\item It does not make use of all the available moment conditions
\item It does not account for the correlation introduced into the errors $\upsylon_{it}$ by first-differencing
\item There are more valid instruments available
\end {itemize}

In this context, \citet{Arellano.Bond.1991}  suggested a Generalized Method of Moments (GMM) procedure that  is more efficient than the estimator proposed by \citet{Anderson.Hsiao.1982}. This procedure  considers the fact that the number of valid instruments is growing in $t$. Because of the dynamic aspect of the estimation and the use of first-differencing, the first and second observations are not usable. For this reason,  the model can be written as:

\[
y_{i3} - y_{i2}  = \rho(y_{i2} - y_{i1}) + (\upsilon_{i2} - \upsilon_{i1})
\]

where $y_{i1}$ is the only valid estimator. For $y_{i4}$ the error is $(\upsilon_{i4} - \upsilon_{i3})$ with  $y_{i2}$ and $y_{i1}$ as valid instruments. In other words, an additional valid instrument is available each time $t$ is increased by 1. The use of additional valid instruments should improve the efficiency of the estimates. In fact, \citet{Arellano.Bond.1991} developed  two versions of the GMM  estimator; the  standard version is called  the one-step version and was designed under the assumptions that the errors are actually heteroscedastic and/or autocorrelated, and an extended version called the two-step version that solves some problems in model consistency that arise when the previous assumptions are violated. The main problem with the Difference GMM estimator is that the lagged first differences are very often weakly correlated with the lagged levels of the dependent variable used as instruments.

An extension of the difference GMM  estimator known as System GMM Estimator will be used in this thesis. The system GMM estimator appeared as a solution to the weakness of the instruments in the difference GMM estimator. In this sense, \citet{Arellano.Bover.1995}  and \citet{Blundell.Bond.1998}  showed that under  a weak hypothesis about the process that generates the data, another moment condition can be added from the  following levels equation:

\[
y_{it}  = \rho(y_{i,t-1}) + u_{it}
\]

with

\[
u_{it}= \mu_{i} + \upsilon_{it}
\]

where it can be showed that the lagged differences, $\Delta y_{i,t-s}$ with $s= 1 ...t-1$, are valid instruments for $y_{i,t-1}$.

This estimator that adds the previous mentioned valid instruments from the equation in levels into the difference model is called the system GMM estimator. This estimator  uses lagged differences of $y_{i,t-1}$, $\Delta y_{i,t-s}$, as instruments for equations in levels, in addition to lagged levels of $y_{i,t-1}$, $y_{i,t-s}$, as instruments for equations in first differences (\cite{Baltagi.2005}).

The system GMM estimator appeared to provide an improvement in efficiency compared to the difference GMM estimator as $\rho \to 1$ and $\sigma^2_{\mu}/\sigma^2_{u}$ increases and a reduction in the finite sample bias. According to the findings presented by \citet{Blundell.Bond.1998}, these improvements in efficiency and precision in the system GMM estimator are larger in "short" panels, which contain data with small $T$ ($T<<N$) and persistent series  (\cite{Baltagi.2005}).


The GMM procedure presents two type of complications to take into account when inferencing:
\begin{enumerate}  [topsep=0pt, partopsep=0pt,itemsep=0.5 pt,parsep= 0.5 pt]
\item If the estimator is consistent, the variance-covariance matrix of coefficients may not, and therefore, the use of robust covariates matrices would be required.
\item GMM estimation is only consistent under the assumptions of no error correlation and of moments' validity.
\end{enumerate}


Several tests are used to determine whether the assumptions of no error correlation and of moments' validity are justified in the corresponding  GMM estimation, the following tests must be considered:

\begin{itemize}  [topsep=0pt, partopsep=0pt,itemsep=0.5 pt,parsep= 0.5 pt]

\item Overidentification Test (Sargan-Hansen):\\
In order to determine whether the assumption of moments' validity really holds, a test for overidentification proposed by Sargan in 1958 and applied to the case of GMM models by Hansen in 1982 will be considered in this paper (\cite{Croissant2}). The null hypothesis in this test is  the validity of the moment conditions.


\item Error Serial Correlation Test (Arellano-Bond):\\
The GMM estimator is also not consistent unless the assumption of serially uncorrelated errors is correct. For this purpose, \citet{Arellano.Bond.1991} proposed a test of error serial correlation   that will also be considered in this paper. In this test the null hypothesis is  that the errors are not serially correlated.

\end{itemize}

Depending on the results delivered by the previously mentioned test, the use of robust covariance estimators may be required in order to perform a valid inference while relaxing the hypothesis of independence and homoscedasticity of the errors. Besides the  general concerns regarding the potential present of heteroscedasticity in the errors, there also are concerns  related to the so-called  "clustering" as individuals and time periods are considered "natural" clusters. These clusters may appear as multiple observations corresponding to the same individual bank or to the same time period tend to share common characteristics that can lead to a violation of the assumption of independent and identically distributed  errors and, therefore, potentially to a biased estimation (\cite{Croissant2}). In the present paper, the model estimations for the standard panel data approach make use of  heteroscedasticity- and clustering-robust  covariance matrices.

	\subsection{Quantile Regression Methods}

 The application of quantile regression methods in this thesis is reasonable when looking at the distribution of NPL, which is characterized by its skewness and the large amount of outliers. The NPL show a negative skewness(left skewed distribution) and the residuals may be non-normal distributed. In samples with many outliers, quantile regression  also shows more robustness  than OLS, and it is semiparametric as it does not make assumptions about the parametric distribution of the errors. Additionally, it is interesting the use of  quantile regressions for the analysis of the impact of the regressors at different points of the conditional distribution of NPL.

In Ordinary Least Squares, the average effect of a set of explanatory variables on the dependent variable  is summarized by the conditional mean function ($E(y|x)$). Similar to the conditional mean function of a linear regression, the quantile regression considers the relationship between the explanatory variables and the dependent variable is defined by the conditional quantile function $Q_{\tau}(y|x)$. In other words, quantile regression  seeks to apply an estimation of conditional quantile functions in which the quantile of the conditional distribution of the dependent variable is modelled as a function
of observed covariates x (\cite{Koenker.1978}). In the same manner that the mean is used to minimize a sum of squared residuals in a standard OLS, this  problem is solved  in a quantile regression by minimizing a weighted sum of absolute residuals (\cite{Koenker.2002}; \cite{Koenker.2005}). Quantile regression methods are a supplement to  the conditional mean approach in OLS as these also allow the estimation of a whole family of conditional quantiles. In this sense, these methods provide  more flexibility to analyze the impact of a set of explanatory variables on the entire conditional distribution of the dependent variable (\cite{Koenker.2002}).


The optimization problem in the quantile regression is dependent on the specific quantile in evaluation. In this context, negative errors suffer higher penalties for higher quantiles, while positive errors are penalized more for lower quantiles. In other words, $Q_{y} (\tau|\enspace x)$ represents the conditional $\tau$-quantile of $y$ given ($x$). The quantile $\tau \in (0,1)$ determines the point of $y$ in which the data is split into proportions under $\tau$ and above $1-\tau$, that is $F(y_{\tau}=\tau$ and $y_{\tau}=F^{-1}(\tau)$. Quantile regression seeks to minimize  the errors $\epsilon_{i}$  with asymmetric penalties, $(1-\tau)\lvert \epsilon_{i}\rvert$ for overprediction and $\tau\lvert \epsilon_{i}\rvert$  for underprediction. In this sense, the quantile regression estimator in quantile $\tau$ is the one that minimizes the following objective function:

\[
Q(\beta_{\tau})  \,=\,  \sum_{y_{i} \geq x'_{i}\beta}^n \, \tau \lvert y_{i}-x'_{i}\beta_{\tau}\rvert \, +\,+ \sum_{y_{i} < x'_{i}\beta}^n \, (1-\tau) \lvert y_{i}-x'_{i}\beta_{\tau}\rvert
\]

The estimator has been shown to be asymptotically normal, and bootstrap standard errors are  often used instead of traditional standard errors.


	\subsubsection{Pooled Quantile Regression Methods}

In a panel Quantile regression, there are pairs of dependent  and explanatory variables $(y_{it},x_{it})$ with $i=1,..., N;\enspace t=1,..., T$, where $i$ denotes  the $ith$ individual and $t$ denotes time. Furthermore, there also are  unobservable individual-specific variables $\mu_{i}$ with $i=1,..., N$, representing individual characteristics. In this method, the quantile regression is based on the conditional quantile of $y_{it}$ given  ($y_{it},\mu_{i}$) and seeks to estimate the partial effect of $x_{it}$ on the conditional quantile. Formally, $Q_{y_{it}} (\tau|\enspace x_{it},\mu_{i})}$ denotes the conditional $\tau$-quantile of $y_{it}$ given ($x_{it},\mu_{i}$), where $\tau \in (0,1)$ is a quantile index (\cite{Koenker.2017}). The basic model is then:

\[
Q_{y_{it}} (\tau|\enspace x_{it},\mu_{i}) \,=\, \alpha_{i} (\tau,\mu_{i}) \,+\, X'_{it}\beta(\tau)
\]

where $\alpha_{i} (\tau,\mu_{i})$ is some unknown function of $\tau$ and $\mu_{i}$, and $\beta(\tau)$ is a dimensional vector of unknown parameters to be estimated in the model. In a more familiar form, the model can also be written as:

\[
y_{it} \, =\, \alpha_{i} (\tau,\mu_{i}) \, + \, X'_{it}\beta(\tau) \,+\, u_{it}(\tau)
\]

where $u_{it}(\tau)$ has a zero conditional $\tau$-quantile given $(x_{it},\mu_{i})$. Under the standard assumption, the conditional quantile restriction is equivalent to:

\[
P(u_{it}(\tau) \leq 0 |\enspace x_{it},\mu_{i}) \,=\, \tau
\]

A panel quantile regression model with individual effects is necessarily nonlinear and for this reason individual heterogeneity can not simply be removed by  first-differencing or transforming the data as it is usually the case in other panel regression models (\cite{Koenker.2017}).


\subsubsection{Fixed Effects Quantile Regression (Penalized FE Method)}

The penalized least squares interpretation of the classical random effects estimator  was developed  by \citet{Koenker.2004} with an estimation technique that offers the possibility of implementing quantile regression models that account for the existence of individual specific effects. This allows the analysis of panel data under less stringent distributional assumptions. These models are also  fit for "short" panel data, where numerous  cross-sectional individuals are observed over short periods of time ($T<<N$). In general, the research on Quantile Regression methodologies for panel data has been experiencing steady growth during the last years as observed by \citet{Koenker.2017}. Formally, the model is defined  as:

\[
Q_{y_{it}} (\tau|\enspace x_{it},\alpha_{i}) \,=\, \alpha_{i} \,+\, X'_{it}\beta(\tau) \quad i=1,..., N;\enspace t=1,..., T
\]

where the individual effect $\alpha_{i}$ does not vary over quantiles (no scalar effect). Furthermore, the following penalized estimator $(\hat{\alpha}(\lambda),\hat{\beta}(\lambda,\tau)$ is considered:

\[
\min_{(\alpha,\beta)} \enspace \dfrac{1}{nT} \sum_{k=1}^q \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{t=1}^T \omega_{k} \rho_{\tau}(y_{it}-\alpha_{i}-X'_{it}\beta(\tau_{k}))+ \lambda \sum_{i=1}^n \lvert\alpha_{i}\rvert}
\]

where $\omega_{1},...,\omega_{q}$ are nonnegative weights and $\lambda \geq 0$ is the penalty level.

The idea in this model is to estimate several $\tau$ simultaneously and restricts the individual effect coefficients  $\alpha_{i}$ to be the same for all quantiles. Moreover, the  estimated  individual effects will shrinks toward zero  as  the penalty level $\lambda$ tends to increase (\cite{Bache.2011}). In this sense, the parameter $\lambda$ allows  a calibration of the importance of accounting for individual effects in the model.

In general, when $\lambda \to 0$ the model is similar to a (weighted) dummy-variable quantile regression and when $\lambda \to \infty$ the model is more like a (weighted) cross-section quantile regression. The penalty allows a "control" over the many fixed effects coefficients by not letting them reach unrealistic large numbers (\cite{Bache.2011}). 

This estimator is expected to deliver results that are between those of a pure dummy-variable  estimator and a pure cross-section estimator. In general, the estimator tends to perform worse in cases where  the fixed effects are assumed to have a scalar effect, that is when the fixed effects show a variation over quantiles (\cite{Bache.2011}).

Table \ref{nlp.selc} contains the results of the estimations and Table \ref{BPF} (appendix) provides the results of multiples tests for the  linear panel models. As observed in Table \ref{BPF}, the Hausman test for the  static panel models suggests that a random  effects approach is more appropriate for households and financial corporations [Prob (Hausman Test) $\geq$0.05], whereas a fixed effects  approach seems to fit better for non-financial corporations [Prob (Hausman Test) $<$0.05]. Moreover, the Breusch-Pagan Lagrange Multiplier and the  F tests for individual effects (p-value $\geq$0.05 ) suggest the existence of panel effects for all exposure types, confirming that the use of panel techniques over simple OLS estimation is also appropriate. As observed in the results of the  Wooldridge's tests, which are specific tests for serial correlation and  unobserved individual effects under null hypothesis of no serial correlation and unobserved effects, there is evidence of serial correlation and the present of heteroscedasticity in the errors for all exposure types (p-value $\geq$0.05). The heteroscedasticity in the errors justifies the use of Heteroscedasticity-consistent  (HC) robust standard errors in the static panel models. 

The Sargan tests, which aim at testing the joint validity of the instruments used for the dynamic panel models, indicates that the null hypothesis of instruments validity can not be rejected at a significant level of 0.01\% [Prob (Sargan Test) $\geq$ 0.01] for all exposure types. Moreover, the results of the Arellano-Bond tests for second-order autocorrelation [Prob (AR(2))$\geq$ 0.05] confirm that the assumption of serially uncorrelated errors also holds. Given the results of the Sargan an Arellano-Bond tests, there is evidence that the GMM estimators are also consistent. Tables \ref{nlp.selc_bk}  and \ref{nlp.selc_75} are also provided  with the purpose of providing additional robustness checks  for the main results in "standard" panel methods. \subsection{Contribution of explanatory variables to NPL}

As observed in the empirical results, the bank-specific and macroeconomic factors considered in this analysis are jointly significant in explaining NPL for all exposures types. These results are also consistent with the case in which only banks with domestic exposures higher or equal to 75\% are taken into consideration. Additionally, the very low p-value of the F-statistics in Tables \ref{nlp.selc},\ref{nlp.selc_bk} and \ref{nlp.selc_75} indicates that the null hypotheses are to be rejected, meaning that  the  joint effects of the explanatory variables on NPL are also  significant. In other words, it can be affirmed that the bank-specific and macroeconomic factors included in the analysis show statistical significance in all models, regression methods and exposure types under consideration,  although the explanatory power largely decreases in models where only bank-specific factors are considered as observed in their adjusted R-squares (Figures \ref{nlp.selc} and \ref{nlp.selc_bk}).

\subsubsection{Macroeconomic indicators}

With respect to business cycle indicators, the unemployment rates and the GDP growth are included in the regressions as explanatory variables. The estimated coefficients for the unemployment rates appear to be very significant and  positively related to NPL for household and non-financial corporations for  all models but insignificant for the case of financial corporations. Moreover, their impact is quite constant for all NPL quantiles as illustrated in the results of the quantile regressions. Regarding the estimated coefficients of GDP growth (GDP\_growth\_perc), these   also seem to be positively related with NPL but with low significance  for all models and exposure types. Thus, it can be concluded that unemployment rates are very relevant determinants of NPL with large and significant coefficients, whereas GDP growth plays a secondary role in these results (Table \ref{nlp.selc} and Figures \ref{qq.graph}, \ref{feqq1.graph}). Moreover, the magnitude of the business cycle indicators  remains quite flat along the distribution of NPL. This characteristic suggests that the impact of macroeconomic indicators on NPL is not affected by the amount of NPL at portfolio level. 

When looking at price levels, the estimated coefficients for the inflation rates are very significant and negatively related with NPL for all models and exposures types, suggesting that the price level is also  very important determinant of NPL and that its impact is marginally increasing along the distribution of NPL in the portfolio (Tables \ref{nlp.selc}, \ref{nlp.selc_75} and  Figures \ref{qq.graph}, \ref{qq.graph.75}). In this sense, these results give empirical support  to the economic argument affirming that higher inflation rates tend to reduce problem loans by cutting down the real value of outstanding loans. 

Similarly, the general government gross debt as a percentage of GDP (Gross\_public\_debt) is also included  in the analysis as an indicator of  the efficiency in the public sector. The estimated coefficients are significant and positively related to NPL, suggesting that the performance of the general government also has a large impact on the development of problem loans at bank level (Tables \ref{nlp.selc}, \ref{nlp.selc_75} and Figures \ref{qq.graph}, \ref{feqq1.graph}, \ref{qq.graph.75}, \ref{feqq1.graph.75}). Interestingly, the estimated coefficients also show a very  significance marginal increase  along the conditional distribution of NPL  as demonstrated in the quantile regressions. This marginal increase indicates that the effect of the public sector on NPL tend to be larger for banks with higher NPL in their portfolios. In this regard, the estimated impact is in line with the theoretical considerations that see a relation between sovereign debt developments and the performance of loan portfolios at bank-level. With respect to indicators regarding Terms of trade,  the estimated coefficients of the  current account balance as percentage of GDP (curr\_acc\_bal)  are significant and positively related to NPL for households and non-financial corporations but not for financial corporations other than credit institutions (Figures \ref{qq.graph}, \ref{feqq1.graph}, \ref{qq.graph.75}, \ref{feqq1.graph.75}). This last result suggests that persistent current account surpluses at national level during the period  2014-2019 may be followed by an increasing amount of problem loans at bank level, whereas the impact along the different NPL quantiles remains quite constant. One possible interpretation is that this relation may be driven by the efforts of many European economies, which excessively accumulated problem loans in their banking sectors, in improving their balance of payments at national level during the last years.

Regarding the characteristics of the legal system, two variables have been included into the models, the strength of legal rights index (Stgth\_legal\_index), which  measures  the strength of collateral and bankruptcy  laws that positively influence the  access to credit (ex-ante), and recovery rates (Reco\_rate\_dol) calculated as recovered cents per dollar in debt. The strength of legal rights index (Stgth\_legal\_index)  shows estimated coefficients  that signal a positive relation  to NPL with low significance. In the quantile regressions, it shows  a close to constant impact along the difference quantiles and statistically significance in the static panel models (Figures \ref{qq.graph}, \ref{feqq1.graph}, \ref{qq.graph.75}, \ref{feqq1.graph.75}). This positive relation holds in all models except for the case of financial corporations other than credit institutions, and it suggests that  the  legal systems of countries with more friendly regulations  for the extension of credit have also been experiencing the larger  accumulation of problem loans during the period 2014-2019. On the other hand, the recovery rates [Reco\_rate] show  a negative and very significant correlation with NPL in all models and exposure types, and with  a similar impact along the conditional distribution of NPL as observed in the quantile regressions (Tables \ref{nlp.selc} and Figures \ref{qq.graph}, \ref{feqq1.graph}). In this sense, the negative relation between recovery rates and NPL also confirms  theoretical considerations arguing that judicial court systems in countries  with more efficient declaratory  procedures in terms of  the final recovered amount are also characterized by a lesser amount of NPL in the portfolios of banks operating in those countries. In general, these results illustrate the importance of the legal aspects with regard to the regulation  of lending activities and NPL resolution to explain the development of NPL at bank-level.

\subsubsection{Bank-Specific variables}  

With respect to the variables regarding the profitability at bank-level, Returns on Risk adjusted Capital (RORAC) and Net Interest Margin (NIM\_tr) provide support for the identification of a causal relation between bank-profitability and problem loans. Returns on Risk adjusted Capital is found to be significant and negatively related to NPL in most models in line with the  "management" assumption presented in the literature, by which highly profitable banks have fewer incentives to enter into riskier activities and, therefore,  highly profitable banks tend to show fewer loans classified as non-performing (Tables \ref{nlp.selc} , \ref{nlp.selc_bk}, \ref{nlp.selc_75} and Figures \ref{qq.graph}, \ref{qq.graph_bk}, \ref{qq.graph.75}). In the case of net interest margins, the coefficients are found to be mostly positive but  with lower significance levels. Furthermore, the impact of net interest margins strongly increases along the distribution of NPL. These coefficients may be explained by the effects of lending policies setting very high (net) interest rates that are not necessarily justified by higher costs of funding on NPL (Figures \ref{qq.graph}, \ref{qq.graph_bk}, \ref{qq.graph.75}). In this sense, higher net interest margins in a bank's balance sheet signal  an increase of problem loans in the long run despite any positive effect in the short run. In this context, the causal relation between bank-profitability and problem loans is negative and in line with the "bad management" hypothesis as long as any improvement in the bank's profitability is not exclusively  explained by higher (net) interest rates over the cost of funding. As indicator of a bank's cost-efficiency, the cost to income indicator  (Cost\_to\_Income) is also incorporated in the models. In this case, the estimated coefficients of the cost to income indicator show a negative correlation with NPL and low significance levels for all models and exposure types (Tables \ref{nlp.selc} , \ref{nlp.selc_bk}, \ref{nlp.selc_75} and  Figures \ref{qq.graph}, \ref{qq.graph_bk}, \ref{qq.graph.75}). As observed in these results, it can be concluded that the cost to income indicator is not  a good determinant of NPL despite previous theoretical considerations in this respect.

With regard to the relation between the size of a bank in terms of total assets and the amount of NPL in the portfolio, the estimated coefficients for the logarithm value of Bank Size (BankSize\_tran) are large, negatively related to NPL and statistically very significant for all models and exposures types. Moreover, the negative impact also shows  very large marginal increase along the conditional distribution of NPL, in which the largest impacts are to be found in the highest quantiles as observed in the results of the quantile regressions (Tables \ref{nlp.selc}, \ref{nlp.selc_bk} and Figures \ref{qq.graph}, \ref{feqq1.graph},  \ref{qq.graph_bk}, \ref{feqq1.graph_bk}). Particularly, these results  offer some empirical support for theoretical considerations  arguing that  the potential gains in terms of bank efficiency  and economies of scale for large banks are expected to have a greater impact on NPL than the effects related to the too-big-to-fail problem explained in previous sections. For this reason, larger banks will tend to show lower NPL ratios in their portfolios compared to smaller banks despite any  size-related incentive  to take additional risks.

As indicators related to bank capitalization, the Common Equity Tier 1 Ratio -CET1- (CET1\_ratio\_tr) and the leverage ratio (LEVER\_tr\_ratio) are also incorporated in the models as explanatory variables.  The estimated coefficients of CET1 are  moderately significant and negative for households and non-financial corporations and not significant for financial corporations, whereas the estimated coefficients of the leverage ratio are positive and mostly insignificant for all models and exposure types (Table \ref{nlp.selc_bk} and Figures \ref{qq.graph}, \ref{feqq1.graph}, \ref{qq.graph_bk},  \ref{feqq1.graph_bk}). Interestingly, CET1, which is the main  core indicator of capitalization for supervisory purposes,  is  significant and negatively related to NPL, while leverage ratios, which are  mostly subject to the bank's discretion, seem to play a secondary role in explaining NPL. In this context, it must also be pointed out that the statistical significance and the values of the estimated coefficients for CET1 significantly increase  for the models that do not include macroeconomic variables (Table \ref{nlp.selc_bk} and Figures \ref{qq.graph_bk},  \ref{feqq1.graph_bk}). Furthermore, the estimated coefficients of CET1 and leverage ratios also present quite constant impact and significance along the conditional distribution of NPL for the quantile  regressions with macroeconomic variables. Interestingly, the marginal  impact  tend to increase at the highest NPL quantiles for the quantile  regressions that do not include macroeconomic variables. In general, the results of the estimated relation between CET1 and NPL is in line with the "moral hazard" hypothesis commented in previous sections by which managers in low-capitalized banks have larger incentives to undertake riskier lending activities that lead to higher NPL in their portfolios. However, the results regarding the impact of leverage ratios on NPL may appear to be explained by the reasoning that higher capitalized banks also have the possibility of implementing more flexible lending policies given previous  compliance with the respective supervisory regulation, and, therefore, a positive relation between the level of capitalization and NPL can also be found.

Additionally, the dynamic model for panel data (GMM) also delivers  the estimated coefficients for the lagged values of NPL by one period. As expected, these estimates are positive, large and statistically significant, demonstrating the importance of the relation between past and present NPL ratios. Particularly, this relation represents the presence of a persistent process, which implies that, all else equal, an  increase (decrease) in the NPL ratio  in a bank's portfolio for one period would likely be followed by another increase (decrease) in NPL ratios  for the next period. In this sense,  the dynamic models  identified the presence of a temporal interdependence  of NPL from different periods within a certain lag.

\subsubsection{Supervision under the Single Supervisory Mechanism (SSM)}

As explained in previous sections, the Single Supervisory Mechanism (SSM) represents the system of banking supervision as one of the first pillars of the European banking union. The estimated coefficients for the dummy coefficient of  membership under the SSM (SSMY) are  negatively related to NPL, large and  statistically significant for  non-financial corporations in the case of random and fixed effects models and also for households in the quantile regressions, whereas the estimated coefficients for financial corporations remain insignificant for all models. Furthermore, the value and sign of the estimated coefficients are quite constant for all quantiles along the conditional distribution of NPL, suggesting that the impact of this variable is similar to all banks regardless of the current NPL in their portfolios (Figures \ref{qq.graph}, \ref{feqq1.graph}, \ref{qq.graph.75},  \ref{feqq1.graph.75}). To some extent, these results present empirical evidence in favour of the statement that  banks under supervision in the context of the  Single Supervisory Mechanism (SSM) tend to show lower NPL ratios. Moreover, the results  may also be interpreted as evidence of the effectiveness of the recommendations and guidances published by the ECB banking supervision  on the development and implementation of NPL strategies as well as indicators of the relevance of the ECB for addressing asset quality issues in Europe. However, despite the validity of the estimation in statistical terms, some caution  is needed when interpreting these results because of the asymmetries presented in the sample (80\%-20\% distribution between SSM members and non-SSM).

\subsubsection{Geographic Diversification Index}

When considering the effects of geographic diversification on asset quality, the estimations results vary among the models under consideration. The estimated coefficients of the Cross-Border Diversification Index (CBDI\_index)  show low and positive  values with statistical significance for all exposure types in the random and fixed effects approach for the models including all explanatory variables, whereas the same are statistically insignificant in the dynamic models (GMM). Once the macroeconomic indicators are  no longer considered in the models, the  estimated coefficients of the CBDI index show different direction and magnitude with negative  and lower values for all exposure types (Tables \ref{nlp.selc}, \ref{nlp.selc_bk}, \ref{nlp.selc_75}).

With regard to the results of the quantile regression, the estimated coefficients of the Cross-Border Diversification Index (CBDI\_index) are  positive but mostly low and statistically insignificant for all exposures types and quantiles  in the models with all variables. However, once the macroeconomic variables are no longer considered as explanatory variables in the models, the  results  changed in terms of sign and value of the estimated coefficients. In this last case, the estimated coefficients of the quantile regression show a negative  and statistically significant correlation between  the geographic diversification and NPL for households and non-financial corporations, whereas in the case of financial corporations these values are close to zero and insignificant. Interestingly, the impact remains constant along the conditional distribution of NPL except for the case of non-financial corporations in which the marginal impact largely increases for higher quantiles (Figures \ref{qq.graph}, \ref{feqq1.graph}, \ref{qq.graph_bk}, \ref{feqq1.graph_bk}).

In general, the overall effects of geographic diversification on NPL are therefore not clearly identified given the divergences in the empirical results  obtained for this indicator. In this sense, an analysis of the effects of geographic diversification on asset quality greatly depends on the degree of incorporation of external factors into the models. However, if the models only include bank-specific factors, the positive effects of geographic identification on NPL are evident in the case of exposures of non-financial corporations. To some extent, this ambiguity is also in line with the literature  as the benefits of cross-border portfolio diversification must be compared against the additional costs derived from the learning curve and other agency problems attached to banking activities abroad.

\subsubsection{Disaggregated loan Aspects: Exposure Types and NPL levels}

As observed in the random/fixed effects  and GMM models for panel data, the estimated coefficients  in households  and non-financial corporations present  in general similarities in direction (+/-)  but differences in magnitude, whereas the estimated coefficients in financial corporations present the greatest divergences in comparative terms with low values and low significance levels. Moreover, there are significant differences in the explanatory power according to the R squared. While non-financial corporations and households are quite good explained by the data  with higher R squared, financial corporations  show lower explanatory power with low R squared. Interestingly,  the largest estimated coefficients in all models are found by non-financial corporations, followed by households and financial corporations, meaning that  the relation between bank-specific/macroeconomic indicators and NPL is more intense for corporates and businesses operating in the non-financial sector (Tables \ref{nlp.selc} , \ref{nlp.selc_bk}, \ref{nlp.selc_75}).

Similarly, the results of the quantile regressions also present significant differences in the estimated coefficients across exposures types. The estimated coefficients in households and non-financial corporations are similar in sign but not in value with non-financial corporations showing the highest impact for almost all explanatory variables. On the other hand, the estimated coefficients in  financial corporations seem to be less reliable, showing very divergent results in terms of direction and values (Figures \ref{qq.graph}, \ref{feqq1.graph}, \ref{qq.graph.75}, \ref{feqq1.graph.75}).

Moreover, the  quantile regressions allow us to assess the effects of the explanatory variables across  our sample of banks with heterogeneous levels of non-performing loans in their portfolios. In this case, the marginal impact of most explanatory variables tend to increase along the conditional distribution of NPL for all portfolio types, showing that the magnitude of the  estimated coefficients is greater  for banks with more non-performing loans  at quantile level in a non-linear fashion (Figures \ref{qq.graph}, \ref{qq.graph_bk}, \ref{qq.graph.75}). In particular, the increase in the marginal impact is quite strong in the case of macroeconomic indicators. In this context,  the magnitude of the estimated coefficients is found to be largest at the upper quantiles. Interestingly, when  only the sample  of banks with domestic exposures greater or equal 75\%  is considered in the estimations, the change of the marginal impact along the conditional distribution of NPL diminishes. This last result explains the somehow flatter curve for the estimated coefficients in comparison to  the estimations that include the full sample. With regard to the levels of significance, the results seem to be the same for each quantile in all models and sample types, where the statistical significance  remains  similar for lower, median and upper quantiles regardless of any divergences in the estimated coefficients.

In general, these results may be explained by the fact that the exposures types considered in this thesis  react  differently to specific sector developments and heterogeneity in credit risks that are also reflected in the NPL.
Moreover, non-financial corporations and households are better explained  by  the selected bank-specific/macroeconomic factors than financial corporations. Given that the selection of the explanatory variables is based on theoretical considerations and other published empirical research studying the determinants of NPL, it is also possible to conclude that   these studies are adequate for explaining problem loans in the  non-financial sector and households, but not for other type of loans such as loans to financial corporations other than credit institutions. Nevertheless, the previous statement does not affect the overall validity of the results due  to the fact that exposures in the non-financial sector and households  usually make up most loan exposures in a traditional bank's portfolio.

Furthermore, the different results at quantile level point out  the presence of large divergences with respect to the determinants of NPL across a sample of European banks  at portfolio level, also characterizing  the heterogeneity in the European banking sector in terms of risk appetite, management strategies and participated markets.

In summary, the presented divergences in the results among exposure types and  quantiles illustrate the importance of an analysis of the determinants of NPL on a disaggregated loan portfolio at bank level in order to obtain a proper understanding of the drivers and evolution of NPL in the European banking system.

\subsection{Research Hypotheses}

Given the empirical results presented in this thesis, the  research hypotheses formulated in the first section are evaluated as follows:


% the following conclusions are presented in the hypothesis questions presented in the first presented in the firwill be considered:
%,leftmargin=5.5mm,2.5m
\begin{itemize}[label= $ $,topsep=0pt, partopsep=0pt,itemsep=3.5pt,parsep= 3.5pt,leftmargin=2mm]

    \item \textbf{$H_{1}$:  NPL are  mainly determined by bank-specific and macroeconomic factors}
    
  Both bank-specific and macroeconomic factors are jointly significant in explaining NPL for all exposures types as observed in the empirical results and, therefore, the inclusion of this class of data for studying the determinants of NPL is vastly justified in our analysis.

    \item \textbf{$H_{2}$:  Business cycle indicators are significantly related to NPL}
    
     Statistical evidence is found in support of the claim that the business cycle also plays an important role in explaining NPL for the non-financial and household sectors but not for financial corporations other than credit institutions. As indicators of the business cycle, two variables are considered in this analysis: GDP growth (annual \%) (GDP\_growth\_perc) and unemployment rates (unempl\_rate\_imf). GDP growth (annual \%) is  only statistical significant for the case of non-financial corporations, in which the estimated coefficients are  positive or negative depending on the  model under consideration. With regard to unemployment rates, a positive correlation with NPL is identified for the non-financial and household sectors, in which the values  are found to be statistically significance.

    \item \textbf{$H_{3}$:  Inflation rates  are significantly related to NPL}
    
    Inflation rates are found to be statistically  significant and negatively correlated for all exposure types. The results point out the relevance of taking a closer look into the price level when analyzing NPL in the banking sector. In this case, the empirical evidence suggests that an increase in the price level may contribute to a  reduction of problem loans probably by cutting down the real value of outstanding loans.
    

    \item \textbf{$H_{4}$:  Public finance indicators are significantly related to NPL}
    
    As an indicator of the efficiency in the public sector, an assessment of the effects of Gross Debt as a percentage of GDP (gross\_public\_debt)  on NPL is also included in this analysis. According to the empirical results,
the estimated coefficients are found to be positively correlated with NPL for all exposure types, and statistically significant for non-financial corporations, households and non-financial corporations other than credit institutions. In other words, it can be affirmed that public finance indicators at country level such as the gross debt as percentage of GDP are significantly and positively related to NPL at bank level given their respective country of origin.

    \item \textbf{$H_{5}$:  Terms of trade indicators are significantly related to NPL}
    
With regard to the terms of trade, the current account balance as a percentage of GDP (curr\_acc\_bal) is also included as a regressor in the models. The estimated coefficients of the current account balance as a percentage of GDP are only statistically significant for the case of non-financial corporations and households. Furthermore, the estimated coefficients are positive, suggesting that trade related indicators may also play an important role  as determinants of NPL in the non-financial and household sectors, but a secondary role in the case of financial corporations other than credit institutions. In this sense, trade surpluses at country level are found to be statistically related with larger amounts of NPL in the banking sector.
 
    \item \textbf{$H_{6}$:  The size of a bank is significantly related to NPL}
    
As an indicator of the size of a bank, a variable for the total assets  at bank level (BankSize\_tran) is incorporated in the models. The estimated coefficients are in this case negative and statistically significant for all exposure types under consideration. In other words, the  size of a bank in terms of assets is significantly related to NPL at bank level. In summary, banks with a larger amount of assets in their balance sheets tend to show lower NPL in their portfolios.

    \item \textbf{$H_{7}$:  Legal aspects of credit access and  NPL resolution are significantly related to NPL}
    
   As indicators of the effects of the legal framework on NPL the following variables were included in our analysis: The Strength of Legal Rights index (Stgth\_legal\_index)  as a measure of the strength of collateral and bankruptcy laws to favour the extension of new credit and Recovery Rate -cents on the dollar-  (Reco\_rate\_dol) as a measure of final recovered amount from NPL after debt restructuring, debt execution (foreclosure) or bankruptcy proceedings. The estimated coefficients of the strength of Legal Rights index (Stgth\_legal\_index)  show a positive  but statistically insignificant correlation with NPL for all exposure types. Therefore, it can be concluded that the strength of the collateral and bankruptcy laws does not seem to play a significant role in explaining the amount of NPL at bank level. All estimated coefficients for the variable Recovery Rate -cents on the dollar-  (Reco\_rate\_dol) show a negative value for all exposure types but only statistical significance  for the case of non-financial corporations and households. For this reason, recovery rates  appear to be an important determinant of NPL in the non-financial and household sectors. In this sense, an increase in the average recovered amount after the conclusion of proceedings in the context of NPL resolution  appears to  be related with a reduction of NPL in the portfolios of banks. In summary, it can be concluded that  certain legal aspects in terms of NPL resolution are significantly related to NPL, while other legal aspects concerning laws favouring  the access to new credit appeared not to be significantly related.

    \item \textbf{$H_{8}$:  Bank profitability and cost efficiency indicators are significantly related to NPL}
    
     Returns on Risk adjusted Capital (RORAC) and Net Interest Margin (NIM\_tr) are included in this analysis  as indicators of a bank's profitability, while the cost to income (Cost\_to\_Income)  is used as an indicator of a bank's cost-efficiency. The estimated coefficients of Risk adjusted Capital (RORAC)  are statistically significant and negatively correlated with NPL for all exposure types  and estimation models. The estimated coefficients of Net Interest Margin (NIM\_tr) show a positive correlation with NPL for all exposure types  with statistically significance for non-financial corporations and households in most models. In this sense, a general improvement of a banks' profitability appears to be related to a reduction of NPL at bank-level once the  effects as long as the improvement is not motivated by higher net interest margins. Furthermore, the estimated coefficients of the cost to income (Cost\_to\_Income) are negative and statistically insignificant for all exposure types. In summary, the empirical evidence suggests a significant relationship between banks' profitability indicators and NPL, while with respect to  the indicator of cost-efficiency a significant relationship could not be identified.
     
    \item \textbf{$H_{9}$:  Bank capital adequacy ratios are significantly related to NPL}
    
  Common Equity Tier1 Ratio-CET1- (CET1\_ratio\_tr) and the leverage ratio (LEVER\_tr\_ratio) are included in this analysis as indicators of a bank's  capital adequacy. The estimated coefficients of CET1 (CET1\_ratio\_tr) are negative and statistically significant for non-financial corporations and households, and insignificance for the case of
financial corporations other than credit institutions. Specially, the magnitude and significance of the estimates greatly increase in models with only bank-specific variables, where the macroeconomic effects are explicitly excluded from the regressions. Regarding the leverage ratio (LEVER\_tr\_ratio),  the estimated coefficients are positive but mostly insignificant for all exposures types. Given these results, it can be concluded that  bank capital adequacy ratios are significantly related to NPL in as much as  the regulatory capital is concerned. In this sense, an increase in the regulatory capital requirements contributes to a reduction of NPL.

    \item \textbf{$H_{10}$: The level of geographic diversification in portfolio is significantly related to NPL}
    
    In order to assess the effects of geographic diversification  on NPL, a Cross-Border Diversification Index (CBDI\_index) is calculated based on the available data on the country of destiny of the exposures at bank level.  The estimated coefficients of the Cross-Border Diversification Index (CBDI\_index)  are either positive or negative depending on the model under consideration. Furthermore, the estimates are statistically significant for non-financial corporations and households but mostly insignificant for financial corporations other than credit institutions. Therefore, although the empirical results suggest that  the level of geographic diversification in a bank's portfolio is a relevant determinant of NPL, there is no  conclusive evidence of whether the final effects on NPL are positive or negative.
    
    \item \textbf{$H_{11}$: Supervision under the SSM  has significant effects on NPL}
    
     A dummy variable for  membership under the  Single Supervisory Mechanism (SSMY)  was created based on the available information on  the banks' country of origin. The estimated coefficients for the dummy coefficient of  membership under the SSM (SSMY) are  negative  for all exposure types and  statistically significant  for non-financial corporations and households. Therefore, the empirical evidence supports the statement that Supervision under the SSM has significant effects on NPL generated in the non-financial and household sectors. For the contrary, these effects appear to be insignificant for financial corporations other than credit institutions. Despite the fact that some caution is needed when interpreting the empirical results due to the presence of some asymmetries in the sample, it can be affirmed that  regulatory supervision under the Single Supervisory Mechanism (SSMY)  contributes favourably to  a reduction of NPL in the European banking sector.

    \item \textbf{$H_{12}$: Magnitude and direction of the effects on NPL varies across exposure types}
    
In this thesis, several models are estimated across three exposure
types: households, non-financial corporations and financial corporations other than credit institutions. As observed when comparing the empirical results across exposure types, the estimated coefficients for households  and non-financial corporations show similarities in direction in terms of positive or negative sign, but differences in magnitude in terms of high or low values. For the contrary, the estimated coefficients for financial corporations tends to diverge from the rest of exposure types in both magnitude and direction. Furthermore, exposures in the non-financial sector present the largest estimated coefficients in all models followed by households.  In this sense, it can be affirmed that the effects of bank-specific and macroeconomic factors on NPL are greatest for  corporates and businesses operating in the non-financial sector, closely followed by the household sector, while exposures in financial corporations other than credit institutions seem to have the lowest impact.

    \item \textbf{$H_{13}$: The effects on NPL vary conditional on the  initial level of NPL}
    
    
In this thesis, the use of quantile regression methodology allows the assessment of the determinants of NPL across  a sample of banks with heterogeneous levels of non-performing loans in their portfolios. According to the empirical results, the estimated coefficients reach their maximum values at the upper quantiles and the magnitude of their marginal impact tend to increase along the conditional distribution of NPL for all exposure types. Furthermore, the estimated coefficients present  similar levels of significance for lower, median and upper quantiles despite any difference in magnitude at quantile level. Therefore, the empirical evidence suggests that the magnitude of the effects of bank-specific and macroeconomic factors on NPL at bank level will diverge conditional on the initial level of NPL in the portfolio. In this sense, these results characterize the importance of taking into consideration the presence of heterogeneity among European banks when analyzing the determinants of NPL.

 \end{itemize}

This evidence is based on the sign and the statistical significance of the estimated coefficients obtained in the regression models, with the exceptions of $H_{12}$ and $H_{13}$, whose empirical evidence is based on comparing the estimated coefficients across exposure types and quantiles along the conditional distribution of NPL, respectively.

\section{Conclusions}

In this thesis, panel data estimation techniques (Random Effects/Fixed Effects/GMM) and quantile regression methods for panel data (Pooled/Penalized Fixed Effects) were applied with the objective of examining the determinants of NPL in the EU-banking sector. For this purpose, the regressions also made use of an extensive, granular dataset on major European banks gathered by the European Banking Authority (EBA) in the context of the EU-wide Transparency Exercise for the period 2014-2019. Particularly, this analysis   takes into consideration disaggregated banks' portfolio characteristics in terms of exposure types (households, non-financial corporations, financial corporations other than credit institutions), geographic composition   of the exposures (country of destiny worldwide), intensity of cross-border diversification and initial levels of NPL. Moreover, the characteristics of the legal system and the regulatory status under the Single Supervisory Mechanism (SSM) are  also analyzed as potential determinants of NPL.
%macroeconomic/bank-specific
In general, a significant correlation between the selected determinants and the development of NPL is observed in the empirical results, pointing out the importance of bank-specific and macroeconomic factors as well as the characteristics of the legal system for explaining the development of NPL in Europe. The signs and significance levels  of the estimates for all the considered models and methodologies are also compatible with the various theoretical arguments presented in section 2 following the available  literature in NPL, with the exception of the effects of GDP growth on NPL, which appear to play a secondary role in this analysis. In light of the magnitude  and statistical significance of the estimated coefficients as well as the general importance and  consistency of these estimates across the different models and exposure types, \textbf{the following indicators are found to be the "most" important determinants of NPL:} \textbf{Common Equity Tier1 Ratio-CET1- (CET1\_ratio\_tr) and Returns on Risk adjusted Capital (RORAC) as the main bank-specific indicators, and inflation rates (infl\_perc), General government gross debt as percent of GDP (gross\_public\_debt) and unemployment rates (unempl\_rate\_imf) as the main macroeconomic indicators. With regard to the characteristics of the legal system, recovery rates (Reco\_rate\_dol)  after NPL resolution proceedings are  also found to be an important determinant of NPL}. 

Regarding the main bank-specific indicators, Common Equity Tier1 Ratio-CET1- (CET1\_ratio\_tr) is the main  capital adequacy ratio for regulatory and supervisory purposes in the European Banking sector, and its estimated coefficients are negative and statistically significant, and therefore it can be affirmed that an increase in the regulatory capital requirements contributes to a reduction of NPL, pointing out the importance of regulatory capital requirements for controlling NPL at portfolio level. Specially, the magnitude and significance of the estimates greatly increase in models with only bank-specific variables, where the macroeconomic effects are explicitly excluded from the regressions. Return on Risk adjusted Capital (RORAC)} is the main indicator of  bank profitability, and its estimated coefficients  are found to be statistically significant and negative. In other words, an improvement of a bank's profitability appears to be related with a reduction of NPL at portfolio level.

Regarding the main macroeconomic indicators, inflation rates (infl\_perc)} remain significance in all models, specially in the models applying quantile regression methodology. In this case, the empirical evidence suggests that an increase in the price level  contributes to a  reduction of problem loans probably by cutting down the real value of outstanding loans. General government gross debt as percent of GDP (gross\_public\_debt)  is also found to be very significant and positively related to NPL, suggesting that the performance of the general government also has a large impact on the development of problem loans at bank level. Unemployment rates (unempl\_rate\_imf)} show a positive correlation with NPL in all empirical models, supporting the claim that the business cycle  also plays an important role in explaining NPL. In this sense, an increase in the unemployment rate in the country where the exposures are located is found to be related with higher NPL for those exposures. With regard to the characteristics of the legal system, Recovery Rate -cents on the dollar- (Reco\_rate\_dol)} appears to be a very important determinant of NPL, where an increase in the average recovered amount after the conclusion of proceedings for NPL resolution  positively contributes to a reduction of NPL. In other words, given the empirical evidence presented in this thesis, it can be concluded that efficient declaratory procedures of NPL resolution play an important role in explaining NPL at bank level.

In this context, the inclusion of variables about supervisory and legal aspects allowed the identification of interesting empirical relations, where structural and country-specific factors regarding NPL resolution and access to credit  are important determinants of NPL. Particular attention is given to the regulatory status under the Single Supervisory Mechanism (SSM), which seems to play an important role in  reducing the levels of NPL in Europe. In this sense, \textbf{the new empirical evidence presented in this thesis suggests that supervision under the SSM contributes significantly to a reduction of NPL in the European banking system}. These results support the claim that a further integration of the European financial sector within the established European institutions would have a positive effect on the fight against  the excessive accumulation of problem loans in the EU-banking sector.

Moreover, the introduction of an index that measures  the intensity of cross-border diversification in a bank's portfolio  as an additional explanatory variable has delivered some interesting insights in the relationship between cross-border exposures and the development of NPL in the European banking sector. In this respect, \textbf{new empirical evidence  is found suggesting that  the level of geographic diversification in a bank's portfolio is in fact a relevant determinant of NPL, although} the results tend to differ across regression methods, exposure types and data samples and, therefore, \textbf{there is no  conclusive evidence of whether the final effects on NPL are positive or negative}.

The results presented in this thesis also provide \textbf{some new empirical evidence  that  highlights the disparities in the  quantitative effects of the various determinants of NPL when  performing a disaggregated  analysis on exposure types}. With regard to the different exposure types, it can be affirmed that the effects of bank-specific and macroeconomic factors on NPL are greatest for  corporates and businesses operating in the non-financial sector, closely followed by the household sector, while exposures in financial corporations other than credit institutions present the lowest impact. \textbf{In this sense, exposures in non-financial corporations are the most sensitive to the determinants of NPL, closely followed by households, whereas exposures in financial corporations other than credit institutions are  poorly explained by these factors}.


Moreover, the results of the quantile regression models also provided \textbf{new insights into  the determinants of  NPL across banks by disaggregating the analysis into groups of banks with different initial levels of NPL in their portfolios}. Given the results of the quantile regression models, \textbf{it is identified that the marginal and absolute impact of most determinants of NPL differs from bank to bank, depending heavily on the original amount of NPL in a bank's portfolio}. In particular, these new empirical results suggest that the magnitude of the impact tends to increase for banks with higher levels of NPL. 

In summary, although  the empirical results for many  of the determinants of NPL considered in the present analysis are in line  with the  general expectations when compared with other results  and  arguments already available in the academic literature, a  serie of "new" and, in this sense, also "unexpected" findings were possible thanks to the novelty  and richness of the EBA's data, which allowed to put the focus of  this analysis on the disaggregated  aspects of NPL in the context of the  most recent European banking system.

\textbf{The "new" findings show that the relative importance of the determinants of NPL depends greatly on the exposure type under consideration, the original amount of NPL as well as the intensity of cross-border diversification in a bank's portfolio. In this sense, this thesis provides some new empirical evidence that further recognizes the heterogeneity in the European banking sector in terms of risk appetite, management strategies and participated markets as well as the need of considering these aspects at a disaggregated level in future empirical research for a proper understanding of the development of NPL in Europe. With regard to the role of  the supervisory system, "new" findings are also provided in support of a further integration of the European financial sector under the Single Supervisory Mechanism (SSM)}. 

%in order to avoid any banking practice that may represent a potential threat to the overall financial stability
In this context, an important contribution also concerns the type of data and the empirical methodology applied for the present analysis.  This thesis has benefited from the  availability of  disaggregated supervisory data disclosed by the EBA on major European banks as part of the EU-wide Transparency Exercise. The use of this data represents quite a novelty given that there are  very few published papers applying it  in the academic research on NPL. In similar terms, the recent application of  quantile regression methodology for panel data in the  analysis of NPL also represents quite a novel contribution. In this regard, \textbf{the present analysis provides a new example of the  potential use of the EBA's data and  a new application of the quantile regression methodology for panel data}.


Furthermore, the empirical evidence presented in this thesis may also be relevant in terms of regulation and policy. For instance, the empirical findings indicate that regulatory capital and profitability ratios  as defined for  banking supervisory purposes are adequate indicators for explaining and predicting the future development of non-performing loans in a bank's portfolio. However, it is advisable for the supervisory authorities to establish  additional indicators aimed at better monitoring the managerial performance in the European banking sector. For instance, a deeper monitoring of the business decisions and practices of a bank's management would contribute to the earlier identification of banks presenting an excessive increase of problem loans. In terms of European policy, it is also  desirable to foster further integration of the European baking system in the context of the Single Supervisory Mechanism (SSM). \textbf{In other words, the supervisory authorities should further  concentrate efforts on procedures and systems for improving risk management tools at a disaggregated level, thus accounting for the large heterogeneity in the European banking system, while promoting at the same time further integration of the European financial sector within the established European institutions}.

Additionally, \textbf{some empirical results or methods presented in this thesis can also be used for the design and calibration  of forecasting models or for bank-stress testing purposes}. In a  bank stress testing framework, the calibration may be performed to test alternative scenarios with regard to the development of bank-specific/macroeconomic indicators and their effects on European banks at portfolio level, contributing to a better assessment of the appropriateness of loan loss provisions in the EU banking sector. Moreover, other related  performance indicators may also be implemented for microprudencial purposes to evaluate the financial risks linked to individual banks showing performance and efficiency problems. Considering that the present analysis is performed on the basis of a disaggregated loan portfolio, the results can also be implemented for stress testing models  that focus on different exposure types.  Moreover,  \textbf{the presented results are also relevant for the design of recommendations to banks on NPL as published  by the ECB and other national supervisory authorities regarding the development and implementation of adequate NPL strategies and Early Warning Indicators (EWI) in the EU banking system}.

The present analyses may be extended in several forms such as the inclusion of a broader choice of bank-specific or macroeconomic  indicators, the extension of the time period by including most recent available data   or a change in the periodicity of the panel data by using narrower time periods on a quarterly or semesterly basis. Furthermore, given the expected  increase in NPL due to the dramatic economic consequences of the Covid-19 pandemic that started in 2020, this analysis can also be extended by  separately considering  the pre-Covid and post-Covid periods and comparing the results  to assess any change in the quantitative effects of the determinants of NPL as well as the overall impact of this unprecedented economic shock on the  future development of NPL in Europe.  

Finally, given the steady increase  in the quantity and quality of the financial data publicly provided by the European Banking Authority (EBA) and other European/International institutions in the context of a constantly changing  legal and economic environment, the development of new perspectives on the analysis of NPL may direct research towards a deeper study of loan-specific characteristics in a legal and empirical framework.

It shows  bank's operating costs as a percentage of net operating income, and it is used as a further indicator of a bank's cost efficiency. \item \textbf{Leverage ratio [LEVER\_tr\_ratio]}
		\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt, partopsep=0pt,itemsep=0.5 pt,parsep= 0.5 pt] 
		\item  \textbf{\textit{Definition}}:\\ 
		the Tier 1 Leverage ratio compares a bank's capital against its total assets. This ratio is used to evaluate how much leverage a bank shows with respect to its consolidated assets. The denominator is composed of Tier 1 assets, which are  assets that can be easily liquidated if a bank needs capital in the event of a financial crisis and the numerator is composed of all the components of the Tier 1 capital, which represents a bank's common equity, retained earnings, reserves and certain instruments with discretionary dividends and no maturity (\cite{EBA6}).

It is an industry-specific profitability ratio for banks and other financial institutions that lend out interest-earning assets. Net interest margin is one indicator of a bank's profitability and growth (\cite{EBA6}).

	 Return On 	Risk-Adjusted Capital (RORAC) is a risk-adjusted measure of returns on investment. It accounts for any expected losses and income generated by capital under the assumption that riskier assets  are expected to be followed by higher expected returns. The definition of RORAC is closely related to the definition of Return on Equity (ROE), with the exception that the denominator is adjusted to account for the amount of risk (\cite{EBA6}).
